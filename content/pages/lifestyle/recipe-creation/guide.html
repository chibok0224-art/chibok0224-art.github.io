What a good recipe development package includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Number of recipes.
  • Testing process.
  • Format: ingredients, steps, times, servings.
  • Measurements: metric, cups or both.
  • Photos, if included.
  • Ownership and originality.
  • Revisions and delivery time.

Ask for a sample recipe written in your preferred format. Consistent structure, clear quantities and simple steps make recipes easier to follow and to publish.

Request notes on substitutions and storage. Readers often ask what to replace or how long a dish keeps, and including the answers improves the recipe's usefulness.

Ask to see recipes the developer has published and try one yourself. Clear ingredient lists, realistic timings and helpful tips show real kitchen experience. Recipes that leave you guessing about pan sizes or doneness are a warning sign.

How to brief a recipe developer

  1. Audience: home cooks, families, professionals.
  2. Cuisine and style.
  3. Skill level and time per recipe.
  4. Products to feature.
  5. Dietary needs.
  6. Measurement system.
  7. Deadline and publishing plan.

Describe the cook you are writing for. A busy parent needs quick, forgiving recipes, while an enthusiast may enjoy longer techniques. Matching the audience is what makes recipes popular.

Ask for recipes that use widely available ingredients in your target market. A brilliant recipe with hard-to-find ingredients will frustrate most readers.

Test one recipe yourself before publishing a series. Cooking it in your own kitchen reveals unclear steps or timing differences that a professional might not notice.

List allergens clearly. Readers with allergies depend on accurate ingredient lists and warnings.

Agree on how ingredient brands are mentioned. Brand campaigns may want their product named in every recipe, while blogs often prefer generic terms. Clear rules avoid revisions and keep sponsored content transparent for readers.

What drives the price

  • number of recipes
  • testing rounds
  • complexity
  • photography or styling
  • nutrition information
  • rush delivery

Simple weeknight recipes cost much less than a cookbook chapter with multiple tests, styling and photography.

Nutrition analysis is often an extra service and should be done carefully.

Red flags

  • No testing.
  • Inconsistent measurements.
  • Recipes copied from other sites or books.
  • Health claims without qualified review.
  • Unclear ownership.

Collect reader feedback after publishing and update recipes if many people report the same problem.

Tips for a smoother project

Ask whether the developer can scale recipes, such as from four servings to a family-size batch. Scaling is not always simple, since baking times and seasoning change, and tested scaled versions save readers from disappointing results.

Plan how the recipes will be presented: a blog with step photos, a printable card, a product label or a video script. Each format needs a slightly different structure and length, and telling the developer early saves rewriting later.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • Every recipe is tested.
  • Measurements are consistent.
  • Ingredients are easy to find.
  • Allergens are listed.
  • I own the recipes.
