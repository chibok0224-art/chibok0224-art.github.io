What an illustrator does

An illustrator creates original artwork by hand or digitally. Styles range from simple line drawings and flat vector art to detailed painting, comics, characters and technical drawings. Illustration brings personality and storytelling: a mascot for your brand, scenes for a children's book, artwork for packaging or editorial images for articles. You hire an illustrator when you need images that do not exist yet.

What a graphic designer does

A graphic designer organizes visual information. They combine typography, color, photos, illustrations and layout to communicate a message clearly. Typical work includes logos, flyers, posters, brochures, social media graphics, presentations, packaging layouts and web graphics. A designer may use stock images or illustrations made by others, and focuses on making the whole piece clear and attractive.

In practice the line is not always sharp. Many designers create simple illustrations and icons, and many illustrators can lay out a poster. Look at what each person shows most in their portfolio; that is usually where their strongest skills are.

How they work together

Many projects combine both. A children's book needs an illustrator for the pages and a designer for the cover typography and interior layout. Packaging may use custom illustration placed into a designed label with legal text and barcodes. A brand might commission illustrated icons that a designer then uses across the website and social posts. When both are involved, decide who leads, share the brand guidelines with each and agree on file formats so the artwork fits the layout.

How to choose

  • You need original characters or scenes: an illustrator.
  • You are publishing a picture book: a children's book illustrator, plus a book designer.
  • You need marketing materials with your text and photos: a graphic designer, such as for flyers.
  • You are launching a product: packaging design, with illustration if you want a distinctive look.

Briefing each one

For illustration, describe the subject, mood, style references, size and final use, such as print or screen. Ask for sketches before final artwork, because changes are easy at the sketch stage and expensive later. For graphic design, provide all text, images, sizes, brand colors and the main message. In both cases, confirm usage rights and the files you will receive, such as layered or vector files.

Timelines and revisions

Illustration usually takes longer than layout work, because each image is created from scratch. A typical process moves from a rough sketch to a cleaner drawing, then to color and final details. Most illustrators include a set number of revisions at each stage and charge for big changes once the final artwork has begun, so give your most important feedback at the sketch stage. Graphic design moves faster, especially when the text and images are ready, but designers also need clear, complete content before they start; changing the text after the layout is finished often means reworking the whole piece.

If you need both, plan the illustration first and the layout second, and leave enough time between them for approvals.

Choosing a style

Illustration style is personal, so look at each artist's portfolio carefully and choose someone whose existing work is close to what you want. Asking an illustrator to work far outside their usual style rarely produces their best work. Designers are more flexible across styles, but still have strengths; check examples similar to your project and audience.
