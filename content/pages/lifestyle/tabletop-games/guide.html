What a good tabletop game package includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Scope: mechanics, rules, content or full game.
  • Player count and play time.
  • Rulebook writing.
  • Playtesting rounds.
  • Component list.
  • Print-ready files, if included.
  • Ownership and timeline.

Ask for a simple prototype you can print and play at home. Playing early versions with friends is the fastest way to find problems.

Request a component list with sizes and quantities. Manufacturers price games by components, and early clarity helps control costs.

Check that the rulebook includes examples of play. Examples help players understand rules far faster than long explanations.

Look at games or rulebooks the designer has worked on and, if possible, play one. Clear setup instructions, sensible turn structures and balanced choices show real design skill, while confusing rules or dominant strategies are warning signs.

How to brief a tabletop game designer

  1. Game idea and theme.
  2. Target players: family, strategy fans, kids.
  3. Player count and play time.
  4. Mechanics you like.
  5. Components and budget.
  6. Publishing plans.
  7. Deadline.

Describe the experience you want: quick and funny, deep and strategic or story-driven. The feeling of the game guides every design choice.

Plan playtests with people who match your audience, including people who have never seen the game. Fresh players reveal unclear rules.

Check component costs early. Extra dice, miniatures and boards raise production prices quickly.

Think about accessibility for players, such as readable text sizes, colorblind-friendly symbols and simple reference cards. Small considerations make the game enjoyable for more people and reduce questions during play.

Ask the designer to prepare a short teaching script or video outline. Explaining a game well is a skill, and a ready-made explanation helps reviewers, stores and new players learn quickly.

What drives the price

  • game complexity
  • number of components
  • rulebook length
  • playtesting
  • art coordination
  • print preparation

Rules editing for a small card game costs much less than designing a full board game with balance testing and print files.

Art and manufacturing are separate costs.

Red flags

  • No playtesting.
  • Rules without examples.
  • Mechanics copied from known games.
  • No component list.
  • Unclear ownership.

Keep versions of rules and components from each playtest. They show what changed and why.

Tips for a smoother project

If you plan to publish or crowdfund, ask about experience with manufacturers and shipping. Production timelines, minimum quantities and packaging affect your plans as much as game design does.

Collect player feedback in a simple form after each session.

Plan the rulebook layout as carefully as the rules. Clear headings, setup diagrams, a turn summary and an index help players find answers quickly during play, which keeps game nights moving instead of stopping for long rule searches.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • Player count and time are defined.
  • I will playtest a prototype.
  • Rules include examples.
  • Components are listed.
  • Ownership is agreed.
