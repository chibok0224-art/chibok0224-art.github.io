What AI writing tools do well

Tools are fast and tireless. They help with first drafts, headline ideas, summaries, rewording for different lengths and turning notes into clean paragraphs. For internal documents, simple product text or a list of ad variations to test, they can save real time. They are best when you already know what you want to say and can judge the result.

What a human writer adds

A skilled copywriter starts by understanding your customer, your offer and what makes you different, then decides what to say and what to leave out. They bring original examples, accurate details from interviews or research, and a consistent voice. They can also push back: telling you a claim is weak, a page is too long or a message will confuse readers. Those judgment calls are hard to automate.

Using both well

Many good workflows combine the two: a person sets the strategy and key messages, a tool helps produce drafts, and a person edits for accuracy, voice and flow. If you hire a freelancer, say clearly whether you accept tool-assisted work and what you expect them to verify. Ask them to check every fact, name and figure, because tools can state wrong details with confidence.

What to give a writer

Share your audience, the goal of the text, the one action you want readers to take, examples of writing you admire, words to avoid and any facts that must appear. A strong brief improves human and tool-assisted work alike. Ask for a short sample on a small piece before committing to a larger project.

Questions to ask a writer

  • How do you research a topic and verify facts before you write?
  • Do you use AI tools, and if so, for what and how do you review the result?
  • Can you show samples written in a voice similar to mine?
  • How many rounds of revisions are included?
  • Who owns the final text, and can I edit it freely?
  • How do you handle sources, quotes and claims that need evidence?

Checking quality

Whoever produces the text, review it with a simple checklist. Are the facts, names, prices and dates correct? Does it speak to your actual customer rather than to everyone? Is there a clear next step for the reader? Does it sound like your business, or like a template? Read it aloud: awkward rhythm and repeated phrases are easy to hear. For anything that makes a claim about health, money, law or safety, have a qualified person check it.

It also helps to keep a short style note: the words you like, the words you avoid, how formal you want to sound and examples of sentences that feel right. Share it with every writer and tool you use, and the results become more consistent over time.

Budgeting for writing

Put your money where the words matter most: pages that bring in customers, such as the home page, key service pages and sales emails. Use cheaper or faster methods for lower-stakes text. A small sample project is a good way to judge fit before you commit, and paying fairly for a few strong pieces usually beats paying little for a lot of forgettable text.

Common mistakes

  • Publishing tool output without checking facts.
  • Using the same generic phrasing as every competitor.
  • Paying for a writer but giving no brief or examples.
  • Expecting one tool to know your brand without guidance.
