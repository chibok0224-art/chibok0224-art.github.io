What a good brand voice package includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Research: interviews, content review, audience.
  • Voice traits with explanations.
  • Tone for situations: sales, support, errors.
  • Word lists: preferred and avoided terms.
  • Before-and-after examples.
  • Format and length of the guide.
  • Revisions and delivery time.

Ask for examples using your real content: a homepage paragraph, an email and a social post rewritten in the new voice. Examples are what people actually copy when they write.

Look at voice guides or messaging work the writer has done for other brands. Strong examples show distinct personalities with practical rules, not generic words like friendly and professional that could describe any company. Ask how the writer gathered input for those projects.

How to brief a brand voice writer

  1. Your brand, values and audience.
  2. Current content you like and dislike.
  3. Competitors and how you differ.
  4. Channels where the voice will be used.
  5. Who writes for the brand today.
  6. Sensitive situations, such as complaints.
  7. Deadline.

Describe your customers and how they talk. A voice that mirrors the audience's language feels natural, while one built only on internal ideas can feel forced.

Include difficult moments in the brief: delays, refunds, outages. A good voice guide shows how to stay human and clear when things go wrong, not only in cheerful marketing.

Ask for guidance on inclusive language and accessibility as part of the voice. Plain words, short sentences and respectful terms make your messages easier for everyone to understand, including people reading in a second language or using assistive technology.

What drives the price

  • research depth
  • number of channels
  • length of the guide
  • examples and rewrites
  • team workshop or training
  • rush delivery

A short voice summary with a few examples costs much less than a full guide with research, channel rules and team training.

Red flags

  • Abstract traits with no examples.
  • A guide too long to use.
  • Ignores customer research.
  • Copies another brand's voice.
  • No guidance for difficult situations.

Share the guide with everyone who writes, and review it after a few months. Update examples as your brand and audience evolve.

Tips for a smoother project

Plan a short rollout once the guide is ready. A brief session or video walkthrough for everyone who writes, plus a one-page summary of the key rules, helps the voice spread through emails, posts and support replies rather than staying in a document nobody opens.

Ask how the voice should adapt across languages and markets if you work internationally. Humor, formality and directness vary between cultures, and a good guide explains which traits stay constant and which can flex so the brand feels natural everywhere.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • I shared content I like and dislike.
  • I described my audience.
  • The guide includes real examples.
  • Difficult situations are covered.
  • The guide is short enough to use.
