The short answer

Yes, Fiverr can be a good place to get a logo, especially for a small business, a side project or a new brand that needs something professional without hiring an agency. It works best when you already know roughly what you want, can describe it clearly and are willing to spend twenty minutes comparing sellers. It works poorly when you pick the cheapest listing, skip the details of the package and hope the designer will work out your brand for you.

When Fiverr is a good fit

  • You need a logo for a small business, a channel, a product or an event, not a full brand system for a large company.
  • You can name your business, describe your customers in a sentence and point to a few logos you like.
  • You want to see a designer's past work and reviews before paying, and compare several styles side by side.
  • Your budget is closer to a freelancer's price than an agency's.

When to look elsewhere

  • You need research, naming, positioning and a complete brand identity coordinated with packaging, signage and a website. That is agency or brand-strategist work, even if part of it can be ordered separately.
  • You expect the designer to decide what your business should stand for. A logo designer can interpret a direction; they can't invent one for you.
  • You need trademark advice. That is a question for a legal professional, not a designer.

Checklist before you order

  1. Portfolio that matches your style. Look for logos close to what you want: minimalist, vintage, playful, corporate. A great retro designer may not be the right person for a clean tech wordmark.
  2. Number of concepts and revisions. Compare what each package includes, not only the price on the first tab.
  3. Files. Make sure the package includes a vector file (SVG, AI or EPS), transparent PNGs and a one-color version. A logo that only exists as a JPG causes problems later. Our article on logo file formats explains what each one is for.
  4. Commercial use and source files. Check whether these are included or cost extra.
  5. Originality. Ask how the designer makes sure the logo isn't based on a template or stock icon. This matters if you plan to register a trademark.
  6. Reviews from similar buyers. Read a few recent reviews from businesses like yours, not just the star average.
  7. A quick message first. Send one specific question before ordering. A clear, prompt answer is a good sign; a copy-paste reply is not.

What to send the designer

A short brief makes a bigger difference than the package you choose. Include your business name and any tagline, what you do and who your customers are, three to five logos you like and one or two you dislike with a reason for each, colors you want or want to avoid, and where the logo will appear: website, social icons, signs, packaging or merchandise. Mention any deadline. If you're unsure how to write one, our article on writing a freelance brief has a simple template.

Red flags

  • Portfolios where every logo looks like it came from a different designer.
  • "Unlimited concepts" at a very low price with delivery in a few hours.
  • Requests to pay or talk outside the platform.
  • No mention of vector files anywhere in the packages.

After delivery

Before you accept the order, check the logo at small sizes, on a phone screen, on dark and light backgrounds and in black and white. Open every file you were promised. Once you accept, further changes usually cost extra, so use your revisions while you still have them. Then store the files in one shared folder with the colors and fonts written down.
