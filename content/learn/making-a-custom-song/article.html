1. The idea and references

Start by describing what the song is for, its mood and its style. Is it a personal release, a wedding song, a birthday gift, a brand anthem or background music for a video? Share two or three reference songs and say what you like about each: the tempo, the vocal style, the instruments or the energy. Note the length you need and any words, names or stories that must be included. The clearer the starting point, the closer the first draft will be to what you imagine.

2. Songwriting

A songwriter creates the lyrics and melody, often starting from your story or ideas. Many deliver a simple demo, such as voice with guitar or piano, so you can hear the structure before investing in production. This is the time to change words, sections or the chorus. Changes later, once instruments and vocals are recorded, cost much more.

3. Production

A music producer builds the arrangement: drums, bass, chords, instruments and sound design. For some genres, a beat maker creates the instrumental. Producers may also hire session musicians to record real guitar, strings, piano or other instruments, which adds feel and character. Ask whether these are included or arranged separately.

4. Vocals

If you are not singing yourself, choose a singer whose voice suits the style. Listen to samples in a similar genre. Send the singer the lyrics, a guide vocal or demo and the instrumental, plus notes on tone and phrasing. Singers usually deliver separate files for the lead vocal, harmonies and backing vocals. Light vocal tuning and editing can make performances more polished while keeping them natural.

5. Mixing and mastering

Once everything is recorded, a mix engineer balances all the parts, and a mastering engineer prepares the final file for release. Our mixing vs mastering article explains the difference and how to prepare files. Listen to drafts on headphones, phone speakers and a car before approving.

6. Rights and credits

Agree in writing on ownership and credits before the work starts. Key questions include: who owns the lyrics and melody, who owns the recordings, how songwriting credits are listed and whether you can release, sell and stream the song. For custom songs bought as gifts, personal use is often enough. For releases or business use, ask for commercial rights. If you use samples or loops, make sure they are licensed for release.

7. Artwork and release

For a release, you will also need cover artwork that meets streaming platform requirements, a distribution service to deliver the song to platforms and a plan for promotion. A short music video or visualizer can help on social media. Be careful with promotion services that promise streams or followers; artificial streams can break platform rules and lead to removal.

Planning time and budget

Costs and timelines grow with each person involved and each round of changes. To keep a project manageable, approve the lyrics and demo before production, approve the instrumental before vocals and approve vocals before mixing. Changing an earlier stage after a later one has started often means paying for work twice. Ask each freelancer how many revisions are included and what counts as a new request. For a gift with a deadline, such as a wedding or anniversary, start several weeks in advance and tell every freelancer the date.
