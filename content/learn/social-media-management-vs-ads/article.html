Social media management

A social media manager plans and creates regular posts, schedules them, replies to comments and messages, and reports on what is working. The goal is a consistent, trustworthy presence that keeps existing customers engaged and slowly attracts new ones. It suits businesses that want to build a brand, share updates and stay visible over the long term.

Organic reach on most platforms is limited, so results build slowly. Management is a long-term investment, not a quick sales channel.

Paid social advertising

A paid social specialist sets up ad accounts and tracking, researches audiences, creates or briefs ad creatives, launches campaigns and optimizes them based on results. Ads can reach exactly the people you choose and drive sales, sign-ups or messages within days. The catch is that results stop when spending stops, and costs can rise if the ads or targeting are weak.

How they work together

The two approaches support each other. A well-managed profile makes ads more credible, because people who click an ad often check your page before buying. Ads, in turn, can show your best organic posts to new audiences. Many businesses start with a modest management plan for consistency, then add a small ad budget to promote the posts and offers that perform best.

Where to start

  • New business with no social presence: start with social media management to build a credible profile.
  • Clear offer and a website ready to sell: test paid social ads with a small budget.
  • Strong products but weak visuals: invest in social media design or UGC ads first.
  • Unsure what to say: a social media strategy session can set direction before you hire anyone ongoing.

Thinking about budget

Management fees are usually a fixed monthly amount based on the number of platforms and posts. Ad campaigns have two parts: the specialist's fee and the money you spend on the platform itself. A common mistake is setting an ad budget so small that the platform cannot learn which audiences respond, so the results look poor and the test ends too early. Ask the specialist what minimum budget they recommend for a fair test, and agree on how long the test will run before you judge it.

Creative content often matters as much as targeting. If your ads use the same tired images, results will fade. Plan to refresh ad creatives every few weeks, either through the specialist or through designers and video creators who produce new versions for testing.

Red flags for both

  • Promises of a specific number of followers or sales.
  • Selling followers, likes or automated engagement.
  • Asking for your personal account password instead of business access.
  • Running ads from an account they own, not yours.
  • No reports or reports without real numbers.

What to share in your brief

Tell the freelancer who your customers are, what you sell, which platforms matter, your brand voice and any examples you like. For ads, add your budget, the action you want people to take and how you will measure success. Give access through business tools such as a business manager rather than sharing passwords, and keep ownership of every account. Agree on a monthly report that shows what was done and what changed, so you can judge results and adjust over time.

Whichever you choose, review results after a fixed period, such as three months for management or a few weeks for ads, and decide whether to continue, change direction or try the other approach.
