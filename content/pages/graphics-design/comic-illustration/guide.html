What a good comic illustration package includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Number of pages or panels.
  • Stages: thumbnails, pencils, inks and colors.
  • Lettering, included or not.
  • Page size and format for print or web.
  • Character designs or a character sheet.
  • Rights for publishing and merchandise.
  • Revisions and delivery schedule.

Ask for rough page layouts, often called thumbnails, before detailed drawing starts. They show panel order, camera angles and pacing, and changes at this stage are quick.

For longer series, agree on a schedule per page or per chapter rather than one big deadline. Regular deliveries let you check consistency and keep the story moving.

Read a full sequence of pages in the artist's portfolio, not just single panels. Good comic artists guide the eye from panel to panel, keep characters recognizable from every angle and make action easy to follow. Those storytelling skills matter more than one impressive illustration.

How to brief a comic artist

  1. The script, page by page, with panel notes.
  2. Characters: descriptions and references.
  3. Style examples from the artist's portfolio.
  4. Format: print, webtoon or social posts.
  5. Color or black and white.
  6. Lettering needs.
  7. Rights and deadline.

Write panel descriptions that focus on what readers must see: who is present, what they do and how they feel. Leave room for the artist's sense of composition; that is part of what you are paying for.

If you plan a series, invest in a character sheet first. Front, side and expression views keep characters consistent across pages and make it easier to work with other artists later.

Read your script aloud before sending it. Long speeches crowd panels and hide the art. Short, natural dialogue usually makes comics stronger and simpler to letter.

What drives the price

  • number of pages and panels
  • level of detail and backgrounds
  • full color versus black and white
  • lettering included
  • character design work
  • publishing and merchandise rights

A short black-and-white strip costs far less than full-color pages with detailed backgrounds and complex action scenes.

Red flags

  • No thumbnail or sketch stage.
  • Characters that change from page to page in samples.
  • Unclear publishing rights.
  • A style that copies a famous comic too closely.
  • No agreed schedule for long projects.

Keep all approved pages, character sheets and source files organized by chapter. Publishers, printers and future artists will need them.

Tips for a smoother project

Plan how readers will find the comic. A webtoon platform, a crowdfunding campaign, a printed book or social posts each need different page sizes and file preparation. Deciding this early lets the artist set up pages correctly and saves reformatting later.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • My script is final, page by page.
  • I will approve thumbnails first.
  • Lettering is agreed.
  • Publishing rights are in writing.
  • The page format fits where I will publish.
