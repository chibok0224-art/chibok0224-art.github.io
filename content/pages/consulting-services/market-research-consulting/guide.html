What a good market research consultation includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Research questions.
  • Method recommendation.
  • Sample plan.
  • Survey or interview review.
  • Analysis approach.
  • Interpretation and recommendations.
  • Timeline.

Ask for a research plan that links each question to a decision. Research that does not change a decision is usually not worth paying for.

Request a review of survey wording. Leading or confusing questions produce misleading answers.

How to brief a market research consultant

  1. The decision you face.
  2. Target market.
  3. What you know already.
  4. Budget.
  5. Timeline.
  6. Existing data.
  7. Stakeholders.

Write down the decision the research should support, such as whether to launch in a new country or raise a price. A clear decision keeps the research focused and stops it growing into an expensive study of everything.

Share existing data, such as sales, reviews and website analytics. It often answers part of the question and helps the consultant design research that fills real gaps.

Agree on what result would change your mind. Deciding in advance what you will do with each possible answer prevents interpreting results to fit a plan you already prefer.

Ask consultants for examples of studies they planned and the decisions that followed. Research that led to clear actions shows practical skill.

Keep raw data and methods documented so results can be checked or repeated later.

Consider the timing of research. Results that arrive after a launch decision has been made are of little use. Share your decision deadline so the consultant can recommend methods that fit the time available, such as quick surveys or a few focused interviews.

What drives the price

  • session length and number
  • review or audit depth
  • written report or roadmap
  • size and complexity of your systems
  • follow-up support
  • consultant experience

A survey review costs much less than designing a full study, managing fieldwork and presenting results.

Consider starting with a few interviews before a large survey. Interviews reveal the right questions and words to use, which makes surveys more accurate.

Plan follow-up research after decisions to see whether the expected effects happened.

Red flags

  • Vague questions.
  • Methods that do not fit the decision.
  • Leading survey questions.
  • Results without implications.
  • Guaranteed outcomes.

Store research plans and results together so future studies build on past learning.

Ask for a short summary of findings for leadership, with the main implication in one sentence.

Ask how results will be presented. Clear charts, short quotes from customers and a one-page summary help teams understand findings quickly and make research far more likely to influence decisions.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • The decision is clear.
  • The method fits the question.
  • Survey wording is reviewed.
  • Results link to actions.
  • Past research is used.
