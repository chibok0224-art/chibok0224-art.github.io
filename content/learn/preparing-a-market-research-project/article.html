1. Start with the decision

Write down the decision the research should support. Examples include whether to launch a product, which customer group to target first, how to price a new service or why customers are leaving. A clear decision keeps the research focused. If you cannot name a decision, the project may be curiosity rather than research, which is fine, but it should be smaller and cheaper.

2. Turn it into research questions

List three to five specific questions that would help you decide. For instance: what problems do small retailers face with inventory, how do they solve them today, what would make them switch and how much do they currently spend on alternatives? Good questions are specific and answerable. A researcher can refine them, but your first draft shows what matters to you.

3. Define the audience

Describe exactly whose opinions or behavior you need to understand: current customers, lost customers, potential customers in a certain industry or region, or decision makers with particular roles. Be honest about how easy they are to reach. Busy professionals and niche groups are harder and more costly to recruit than general consumers.

4. Choose methods

Different questions need different methods:

  • Desk research: reviewing existing reports, statistics, competitor websites and reviews. Quick and affordable for market size and competitor landscape.
  • Interviews: in-depth conversations that reveal needs, motivations and language. Useful early, when you do not yet know what to ask in a survey.
  • Surveys: structured questions to measure how common opinions or behaviors are across many people.
  • Usability and concept testing: watching people react to a product, prototype or idea. See our user testing guide.
  • Data analysis: using your own sales, website or customer data to find patterns.

Many projects combine methods, for example interviews first, then a survey to measure what the interviews uncovered.

5. Share what you already know

Provide previous research, customer feedback, sales data, support tickets and competitor lists. This prevents the researcher from repeating work and helps them focus on gaps. If you have your own data to analyze, a data analyst can work alongside the researcher.

6. Avoid common pitfalls

Research can mislead if questions are leading, participants are not representative or only friendly customers are asked. Ask the researcher how they will reduce bias, how participants will be selected and what the limits of the findings will be. Be open to results you do not expect; the value of research lies in learning something new.

7. Agree on outputs

Describe what you want at the end: a short summary for decision makers, a detailed report, charts, interview notes, survey data or a presentation. Ask for clear recommendations linked to your original questions, as well as the evidence behind them. Agree on how participant data will be stored and anonymized.

8. Act on the findings

Plan a session with your team to review the findings soon after delivery. Decide what you will change, what you will test next and what still needs more evidence. Research that sits in a folder has no value. Many businesses also schedule small, regular research activities, such as a few customer interviews each quarter, rather than one large study every few years. This keeps decisions grounded in current customer needs and makes each project easier, because you build on what you learned before.

Preparation checklist

  • Decision the research supports.
  • Three to five research questions.
  • Target audience and how to reach them.
  • Methods discussed and agreed.
  • Existing data and research shared.
  • Approach to bias and limitations explained.
  • Outputs, timeline and data handling agreed.
