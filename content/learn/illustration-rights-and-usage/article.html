Personal and commercial use

The first distinction is between personal and commercial use. Personal use covers things like a portrait hung at home or a gift. Commercial use covers anything connected to a business or sales: a website, advertising, packaging, a book cover, products for sale or social media for a brand. Many illustrators price commercial use higher, because the artwork creates value for the buyer. On freelance platforms, commercial use is often an extra option in the package, so check before ordering.

License or full ownership

A license gives you permission to use the artwork in agreed ways, while the artist keeps the copyright. Full ownership, sometimes called a copyright transfer or buyout, gives you the rights to the artwork itself. Licenses are common and often fine. If the illustration will become a central part of your brand, such as a mascot, a character in a book series or artwork on a product line, ask for broad rights or ownership so you are not restricted later.

Exclusive or non-exclusive

Exclusive rights mean the artist will not sell or license the same artwork to anyone else. Non-exclusive rights mean they could. For custom commissions, exclusivity is usually expected, but confirm it, especially for characters, patterns and designs that could easily be resold. Ask the artist to confirm in writing that the artwork is original and not reused from previous work.

Be specific about where it will be used

List every use you expect: website, social posts, paid ads, print materials, a book interior or cover, merchandise, packaging or animation. If you might use the artwork on t-shirts and merchandise, say so now. Products for sale are often treated separately from marketing use. If you plan to animate a character later, mention it, because you will need suitable files and permission to adapt the artwork.

Source files and edits

Final images are usually delivered as high-resolution PNG or JPG files. If you want layered source files, vector files or separate character elements, ask in advance; they may cost extra. Also ask whether you may edit the artwork, such as changing text, colors or cropping it for new formats, or whether changes should go back to the artist.

Credit and portfolio

Many artists like to be credited when their work is published and to show it in their portfolio. Both are reasonable, but if you need confidentiality, such as for an unreleased product, agree on a date after which the artist may share it. If credit is expected, agree on the wording and where it appears.

Questions about AI tools

If it matters to you how the artwork is made, ask directly whether any AI image tools are used and for which parts. Rules about registering and protecting AI-generated images are still developing in many places, which can matter for brand characters or books. A clear answer up front avoids surprises later.

If your plans change later

If you are unsure what you will need, say so and ask the artist to suggest options. Many illustrators offer a basic commercial license with the option to extend it later, which can be a fair starting point.

What to agree in writing

  • Personal or commercial use, and every planned use listed.
  • License or full ownership, and whether it is exclusive.
  • Merchandise, packaging, book or animation rights if needed.
  • File types, including source or vector files.
  • Whether you may edit or adapt the artwork.
  • Credit, portfolio use and any confidentiality period.
  • How the artwork is created, if that matters to you.
