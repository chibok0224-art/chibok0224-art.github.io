1. Purpose and reader

Decide why you need the plan and who will read it. A plan for a bank loan, a grant or startup program, potential partners, investors or your own internal planning each has different emphasis and length. Many lenders and programs publish required formats or templates; get these before the writer starts. Knowing the reader also helps the writer choose the right tone and level of detail.

2. Your business model

Write down in plain language what you sell, to whom, how you reach customers, how you make money and what makes you different. Include prices, sales channels and any key partners or suppliers. If parts are still uncertain, say so; a good consultant can help you think them through, but the decisions must be yours. A business consultant may be useful if the model itself needs work before writing.

3. Market and competition notes

Gather what you know about your market: who your customers are, how many there might be in your area or segment, what trends affect them and who your competitors are. Share any research, customer conversations or sales data. If you need deeper evidence, consider a market research project first. Our market research preparation article explains how.

4. Operations and team

Describe how the business runs: location, equipment, suppliers, processes, staffing plans and key milestones. Provide short bios of the founders and key team members, highlighting relevant experience. Note any licenses, permits or regulations that apply, and get professional advice where needed. Writers can present this clearly, but they rely on your accurate information.

5. Financial inputs

Financial projections are built from assumptions: prices, expected sales volumes, costs, hiring plans, funding needs and timing. Prepare these assumptions with as much evidence as you can, such as supplier quotes, current sales or comparable businesses. A financial modeling freelancer can turn them into forecasts and statements, but they should explain how each number is calculated. For tax, accounting, legal and investment questions, consult qualified professionals; a business plan writer is not a substitute for that advice.

6. Working with the writer

Expect an intake questionnaire or interview, followed by an outline, a first draft and revision rounds. Answer questions in detail and quickly. Review drafts carefully for accuracy and make sure the plan sounds like your business, not a generic template. Agree on deliverables, such as an editable document, a financial spreadsheet and perhaps a short summary or presentation.

7. Keep it realistic

Readers such as lenders and program reviewers see many plans and quickly notice unrealistic projections or vague claims. A credible plan explains risks and how you will handle them, uses evidence for key numbers and sets achievable milestones. Be cautious of anyone who promises funding approval or guaranteed results.

8. Keep the plan alive

A business plan is most useful when it is revisited. Once a quarter, compare actual results with your projections, note what was different and why, and update your assumptions. This turns the plan into a management tool rather than a document written once for a single application. Ask for an editable version of the document and the financial spreadsheet so you can update them yourself, and keep a short log of major changes. When you next approach a lender, partner or program, an up-to-date plan backed by real results is far more convincing than a polished but outdated one.

Preparation checklist

  • Purpose, reader and any required template.
  • Business model described in plain language.
  • Market, customer and competitor notes.
  • Operations, milestones and team bios.
  • Financial assumptions with supporting evidence.
  • Process, deliverables and review time agreed.
  • Professional advice arranged for legal, tax and finance questions.
