What a good pitch deck package includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Story outline before design.
  • Number of slides.
  • Content writing or editing.
  • Charts for data.
  • Design and brand.
  • Editable files.
  • Revisions and delivery time.

Approve the story outline before slides are designed. A clear narrative matters more than visual polish.

Ask for a short version for email and a presentation version for meetings. Each has different needs.

Look at decks the expert has worked on and ask what happened next, such as meetings booked or programs joined. Clear stories and honest numbers in their samples show they understand what readers look for, not only how to make slides attractive.

How to brief a pitch deck expert

  1. Your business and stage.
  2. Audience: investors, grants, partners.
  3. Key numbers and sources.
  4. Traction and milestones.
  5. Team.
  6. The ask.
  7. Deadline.

Use real, sourced numbers. Inflated market sizes or projections damage credibility immediately with experienced readers.

Show traction honestly, even if small. Real customers, pilots or waitlists are more convincing than big promises.

Practice presenting with the deck and adjust slides that are hard to explain.

Think about the readers. Investors, grant panels and corporate partners care about different things, such as growth potential, social impact or strategic fit. Telling the expert who will read the deck helps them emphasize the right points.

What drives the price

  • number of slides
  • content writing
  • data visualization
  • custom design
  • versions
  • rush delivery

Redesigning an existing deck costs much less than building a new story, content and design from scratch.

Red flags

  • Promises of funding.
  • Inflated numbers.
  • Design without story.
  • No editable files.
  • Templates that ignore your brand.

Keep a master deck and update numbers before every meeting.

Tips for a smoother project

Prepare answers to likely questions in an appendix. Backup slides on unit economics, competition and risks show you have thought deeply about the business.

Keep the design simple and readable. Large numbers, short headlines and one idea per slide help readers follow the story, whether they are listening to you present or reading the deck alone on a phone between meetings. Busy slides with long paragraphs often lose attention within the first minute.

Ask for consistency between the deck and any other documents you send, such as a one-page summary or business plan.

Ask the expert to review your deck from the reader's point of view: what questions would a skeptical reader ask on each slide? Answering those questions in the deck, or preparing answers for the meeting, makes you far more convincing and confident.

Questions to ask before you order

  • Have you made decks for my stage and industry, and can you show examples?
  • Will you help shape the story and slide order, or only design my existing content?
  • How will you present my numbers so they are clear without exaggerating them?
  • Will I receive editable files in the presentation software I use?
  • How many revision rounds are included, and can you make a short version to send by email?

Rehearse the deck out loud before you approve it. If a slide needs a long explanation, it usually needs simplifying, and investors will notice the same thing.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • The story is approved first.
  • Numbers are real and sourced.
  • Traction is shown honestly.
  • I get editable files.
  • Nobody promised funding.
