1. Decide the goal

Choose the main thing the series should achieve. For a shop it might be a first purchase. For a service business it could be a booked call. For a publisher it might be regular reading. A single clear goal gives the series a direction and lets you judge success. Decide also how a person enters the series: signing up on the website, downloading a guide, creating an account or buying.

2. Know the new subscriber

Think about why people join. They may want a discount, advice or updates. Write down their questions and worries at that moment: Is this brand trustworthy? Will the product suit me? How does it work? The best welcome emails answer these in order. Use real customer questions and reviews as a source of material.

3. Outline the emails

A simple structure works well. The first email delivers the promise and sets expectations. The second tells your story or explains what makes you different. The third answers common questions or shows how to get started. The fourth shows proof such as reviews or results. The last invites the next step, perhaps with a gentle reminder or offer. Share your outline with the freelancer and invite suggestions.

4. Plan the timing

Decide when each email is sent: the first right away, then spaced over days. Spacing too tightly feels pushy, and too loosely loses attention. Consider time zones and whether the sequence should change based on actions, such as stopping once someone has bought. Ask how the platform handles these rules and test them before launch.

5. Share your voice and look

Provide brand colors, fonts, the logo and examples of emails you like and dislike. Describe the tone: friendly, expert, playful or formal. Provide any wording to avoid and facts that must be correct, such as shipping times and return policies. Ask for mobile-friendly designs and a plain version, since many people read on phones.

6. Respect consent and rules

People should have agreed to receive your emails, and every message needs a clear unsubscribe link and your business details. Rules differ by country, so check what applies to your subscribers and ask a qualified professional if you are unsure. Ask the freelancer to set up the sign-up form and confirmation in line with those rules and to keep records of consent.

Questions to ask the freelancer

  • Can you show welcome series you wrote or set up, with results if available?
  • How many emails and revisions are included?
  • Will you set up the automation in my account and test it fully?
  • How will you design emails for phones and different email apps?
  • Which results will you track, and how will we improve the series?
  • Who owns the text, templates and the account?

Testing and improving

Before launch, sign up with a test address and go through the whole series. Check the timing, the links, the unsubscribe step and how each email looks on a phone. After a few weeks, review which emails get clicks and which lose people. Change one thing at a time, such as a subject line or the offer, and note the results. Plan a review every few months, since products, prices and customer questions change.

Budget and timing

A short welcome series with a few emails can be written and set up quickly, while a series with several paths based on behavior takes longer. Ask for a fixed scope listing the emails, templates and automation rules. Allow time for your own review of every message, since you must be comfortable with each promise and each fact. Plan to revisit the series after a month of real data, and budget for a small round of improvements.

Common mistakes

  • Sending long emails with many different requests.
  • Forgetting to stop the series after someone buys.
  • Not testing links, timing and the mobile view.
  • Using promises or discounts that the business cannot honor.
