What a good music lesson package includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Instrument or skill and levels.
  • Lesson length and frequency.
  • Styles and repertoire.
  • Practice plans and materials.
  • Recorded feedback, if offered.
  • Technical setup guidance.
  • Cancellation rules and pricing.

Ask for a trial lesson and check the audio and video setup together. Being able to hear and see clearly in both directions is essential for correcting technique.

Ask teachers how they structure progress over the first few months. A clear path, such as basic chords first, then rhythm patterns, then full songs, shows they plan lessons thoughtfully rather than improvising each week.

How to brief a music teacher

  1. Instrument and level.
  2. Goals: songs, technique, exams, performing.
  3. Music styles you like.
  4. Practice time available.
  5. Equipment you have.
  6. Schedule and time zone.
  7. Budget.

Share a few songs you dream of playing. Teachers can build technique exercises around them, which keeps practice meaningful and motivating.

Record yourself between lessons and send short clips if the teacher offers feedback. Listening back helps you notice timing and tone issues you miss while playing.

Agree on a practice routine you can actually keep, such as twenty minutes a day. Consistency matters more than long weekend sessions.

Mention any pain or tension while playing. Good teachers adjust posture and technique early to prevent injuries.

Consider whether you want to learn to read music. Some styles and goals benefit from notation, while others rely on ear training and tabs. Tell the teacher your preference so lessons build the skills you actually want.

What drives the price

  • instrument and level
  • teacher experience
  • lesson length
  • recorded feedback
  • exam preparation
  • production software lessons

Casual lessons for beginners cost less than advanced lessons with exam preparation or professional production coaching.

Packages often lower the price per lesson; check how long they stay valid.

If you are buying lessons for a child, ask how the teacher keeps young students engaged. Short activities, games, songs they know and praise for small steps keep children motivated, and a parent nearby during early lessons helps with setup and practice at home.

Red flags

  • Poor audio or video setup.
  • No practice guidance.
  • Ignores your musical goals.
  • No correction of posture or technique.
  • Unclear cancellation rules.

Track your progress with recordings every month. Hearing improvement over time is one of the best motivators.

Set small performance goals, such as playing a song for friends.

Think about performance goals, even small ones. Playing for family, joining an online open mic or recording a cover gives your practice a purpose. Teachers can help you prepare and choose pieces that show your progress.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • Audio and video work well both ways.
  • Lessons include songs I like.
  • I have a realistic practice routine.
  • Technique and posture are checked.
  • I record my progress.
