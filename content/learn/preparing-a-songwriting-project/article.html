1. Decide what the song is for

Name the purpose: a wedding or birthday gift, a brand jingle, a theme for a video or podcast, a game, a campaign or a track for your own release. The purpose shapes length, tone and how formal the lyrics should be. A gift may need personal names and stories, while a brand song needs a catchy message that fits your audience. Say where the song will be played and for how long, since that may affect the license you need.

2. Describe style and mood

Words like upbeat or emotional are a start, but examples are better. Send links or titles of three to five songs that capture the sound you want, and say what you like about each: the rhythm, the voice, the instruments or the energy. Mention anything you want to avoid. If you are unsure about the genre, describe the feeling you want listeners to have and let the songwriter propose options.

3. Prepare lyric ideas

Write down the main message in one sentence. Add details that make a song personal and memorable: names, places, shared stories, small jokes or slogans. For a brand song, list the key points and phrases that must appear and any words that are not allowed. If you have written lyrics yourself, share them and say whether they can be changed. Songwriters usually work better with a clear brief and some freedom than with a rigid script.

4. Choose vocals and production level

Decide whether you want a finished song with vocals and instruments, a simple demo, sheet music or just lyrics and melody. Ask whether the singer is the songwriter or someone else, and whether you can hear voice samples. Mention language, vocal type and any limits, such as a clean version without explicit words. Higher production levels cost more and take longer, so match the choice to your needs.

5. Settle rights before you start

Ask exactly what you will receive: the audio files, the lyrics, the right to use the song commercially and the right to publish it on streaming platforms. Ask whether credit is required and whether the songwriter may reuse the music. If you plan to release the song or use it in ads, get the license terms in writing. Make sure the song does not copy an existing tune; ask the songwriter to confirm that it is original.

6. Plan revisions and delivery

Agree how many rounds of changes are included and what can be changed at each stage. Changing the story or style late can mean starting over. Ask for the file types you need, such as high-quality audio, an instrumental version and lyrics as text. Keep copies of everything and note the date of delivery.

Questions to ask the songwriter

  • Can you share previous songs in a style close to mine?
  • Is a demo or a lyrics preview included before the full production?
  • How many revision rounds do I get?
  • Do I receive full ownership or a license, and in writing?
  • What is the delivery time and what do you need from me?
  • Do you provide instrumental and clean versions?

Budget and timing

Prices depend on the level of production: lyrics only, a demo with a simple recording, or a finished song with a singer and instruments. Decide what level you need and ask for a clear list of what each package includes. Give yourself time, because writing, recording and revisions take longer than most people expect, especially for gifts and events with a fixed date. Share your deadline at the start so the songwriter can say honestly whether it is possible.

Common mistakes

  • Giving only vague words such as nice or catchy and no examples.
  • Not asking about rights before paying.
  • Making big changes late in the process.
  • Expecting a fully produced track from a lyrics-only package.
