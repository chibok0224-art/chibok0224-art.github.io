1. Define what you want from search

Start with the business goal, not the technical wish. Do you want more phone calls from people nearby, more orders for specific products, more sign-ups, or more visitors who read your articles? A local service, an online shop and a publisher need very different work. Write the goal in one sentence and name the pages that should bring the results. The auditor can then judge every finding by whether it helps that goal.

2. List your most important pages

Name the pages that matter most, such as your main service pages, your best-selling categories or your key articles. Include their addresses. Mention pages that you plan to add or retire. If you have recently redesigned the site, moved to a new address or changed platforms, say so, because such changes often cause search problems that are easy to fix once they are known.

3. Prepare access and data

Set up or confirm your free search reporting tool and your analytics, since they show which queries bring visitors and which pages have problems. Give the specialist read-only access through the tools' own user settings, never by sharing a password. If you have a list of your main products or services, previous SEO reports or notes about past work, share them. Past mistakes, such as bought links or copied text, should be disclosed so they can be handled properly.

4. Name your competitors

List a few businesses you compete with in search, including those that appear above you when you search for your own services. Note any that you consider especially strong. Competitor comparison shows which topics you are missing and what kind of content search engines seem to reward in your field.

5. Agree the scope and deliverables

Ask exactly what you will receive. A useful audit includes a clear summary of the top issues in order of importance, specific pages and examples for each issue, an estimate of effort and expected impact, and a plan for what to do first. Ask whether it covers technical checks, content and keywords, local listings if you have a physical location, and links from other sites. Ask for a call or recorded walkthrough so that you can ask questions.

6. Decide who makes the changes

Some fixes are simple, such as page titles. Others need a developer, a writer or a designer. Decide who on your side will handle each type of change, and ask the specialist to write recommendations so that your developer or writer can follow them. If you want the specialist to implement the work, ask for a separate quote and agree on what will be changed so that nothing important is altered without your approval.

Questions to ask the specialist

  • How do you decide which findings matter most for my site?
  • Which tools and manual checks do you use?
  • Will I receive a prioritized action list, not only a raw report?
  • How do you avoid methods that could harm my site, such as bought links?
  • How will we measure progress, and over what period?
  • What will you need from my developer or writer?

Budget and timing

Audits differ in depth: a quick review of a small site is very different from a full study of a large shop. Ask for a clear scope, a delivery date and what happens if your site is larger than expected. Allow time afterward to act on the findings, since the audit only pays off when the work is done. Plan a follow-up check after a few months to see what changed and what to do next.

Common mistakes

  • Paying for an automated report that nobody has read.
  • Expecting quick ranking changes right after the audit.
  • Fixing small details while ignoring the pages that bring the money.
  • Hiding past problems from the specialist.
