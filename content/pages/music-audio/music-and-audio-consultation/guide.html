What a good music consultation includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Format and length.
  • Materials reviewed.
  • Areas covered: song, mix, release, career.
  • Written notes or recording.
  • Follow-up questions.
  • Consultant experience.
  • Pricing.

Ask for time-stamped notes on your tracks. Specific comments like the vocal drops in level at 1:20 are far more useful than general impressions.

Request a short action list after the session, ranked by impact.

Look at the consultant's experience in your genre and career stage. Advice from someone who has released, mixed or managed music like yours is far more relevant than general industry opinions. Ask what artists or projects they have worked with.

How to brief a music consultant

  1. Your tracks and versions.
  2. Genre and influences.
  3. Goals: release, sync, live, career.
  4. Specific questions.
  5. Current results: streams, feedback.
  6. Equipment, for studio advice.
  7. Timeline.

Write focused questions, such as is my chorus strong enough or does my mix compete with similar releases. Clear questions lead to clear answers.

Share reference tracks you aim to match. Comparing your work to references gives the consultant a realistic benchmark.

Be wary of advice that always leads to buying the consultant's other services. Good advice stands on its own.

Ask how the consultant stays current. Streaming, social platforms and release strategies change quickly. Advice based on current practices is more valuable than tactics that worked several years ago.

What drives the price

  • session length
  • number of tracks
  • written reports
  • consultant reputation
  • follow-up sessions
  • rush delivery

A single track review costs much less than ongoing mentoring with regular sessions and release planning.

Red flags

  • Vague feedback.
  • Guarantees of deals or playlists.
  • Pressure to buy more services.
  • No notes or recording.
  • Ignores your goals.

Revisit the notes before your next release to see what improved and what still needs work.

Tips for a smoother project

Set one or two goals for the next few months based on the advice, such as finishing an EP or improving mixes. Concrete goals turn feedback into progress.

Record the session or ask for written notes, then review them a day later. Fresh ears help you decide which suggestions to try first and which questions to ask in a follow-up.

Consider sharing works in progress, not only finished songs. Early feedback on arrangement and direction can save you time and money before mixing and mastering begin.

If you work with a band or team, involve them in the session or share the notes. Shared understanding of the feedback helps everyone pull in the same direction and avoids disagreements about what to change.
