1. Choose the right process

List tasks your team repeats every day or week and estimate the time each takes. Good candidates are rule-based, frequent and predictable: copying form submissions into a CRM, creating invoices from orders, sending reminders, updating stock levels or posting notifications to a team chat. Tasks that require judgment, change often or happen rarely are less suitable. Start with one or two processes where saved time or reduced errors will be noticeable.

2. Map the current workflow

Write down each step as it happens today: what starts the process, which tools are involved, what information moves where, who approves what and what happens in unusual cases. A simple numbered list or flowchart is enough. Mapping often reveals unnecessary steps that can be removed before automation, which makes the final automation simpler, cheaper and more reliable.

3. List tools and access

Name every tool in the workflow, such as forms, spreadsheets, email platforms, CRM, accounting software, online store or project management tools. Check that each has an integration or API, and what plan level is required. Create a dedicated account or user for the automation where possible, rather than connecting it to a personal login. This keeps automations running when staff change and makes access easier to manage and revoke.

4. Define data rules

Automation depends on consistent data. Decide which fields are required, how names, dates and phone numbers should be formatted, and what should happen with duplicates. If the data includes personal or sensitive information, note where it may be stored and who may see it. A freelancer experienced in integrations will ask these questions, but having answers ready speeds up the project and avoids surprises.

5. Plan for errors

Every automation will eventually meet unexpected data or a tool that is temporarily unavailable. Decide who should be notified when something fails and how. Ask for logs that show what ran and what did not. Avoid automations that fail silently, because lost orders or missing leads can go unnoticed for weeks.

6. No-code tool or custom code

Visual automation tools connect many popular apps and are easy for non-developers to adjust. They suit most small business workflows. Custom code or scripting may be better for high volumes, complex logic, tools without ready-made connectors or where monthly tool costs would become high. Some projects combine both. For tasks like summarizing messages or sorting requests, AI integration can add intelligence, ideally with human review for important decisions.

7. Understand running costs

Ask for an estimate of ongoing costs: automation tool subscriptions, usage-based fees per task, AI usage and hosting for custom code. Compare these with the time saved. A workflow that saves a few minutes a month may not be worth a monthly subscription, while one that saves hours each week usually is.

8. Test and document

Test with realistic examples, including unusual cases, before switching the automation on fully. Run it alongside the manual process for a short period if the stakes are high. Ask the freelancer to document what each automation does, which accounts it uses and how to pause or change it. Keep this documentation where your team can find it.

9. Keep ownership

Make sure automations live in accounts your business owns, not the freelancer's personal account. Otherwise, workflows may stop when the engagement ends.

Preparation checklist

  • One or two repetitive processes chosen.
  • Current workflow mapped step by step.
  • Tools listed, integration options checked, automation accounts created.
  • Data rules and privacy requirements defined.
  • Error alerts and logs planned.
  • Approach and running costs agreed.
  • Testing plan and documentation requested.
