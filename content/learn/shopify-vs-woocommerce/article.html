Shopify

Shopify is an all-in-one store platform. You choose a theme, add products and connect payments, and Shopify handles hosting, security, updates and checkout. A large app store adds features such as reviews, subscriptions and email marketing. It suits sellers who want to focus on products and marketing rather than technology.

Costs include a monthly plan, transaction or payment fees and paid apps. You work within Shopify's structure; most needs are covered, but very unusual features may be hard to build.

WooCommerce

WooCommerce adds store features to WordPress. Because you host it yourself, you control everything: design, data, plugins and custom features. It suits businesses that already use WordPress, want content and store in one place, or need custom functionality.

The responsibility is yours: hosting quality, updates, backups, security and making sure plugins work together. Many store owners pay a freelancer for regular maintenance.

Both platforms support the basics most stores need: product variants, discount codes, shipping rules, taxes and customer accounts. The differences show up in how much setup and upkeep you take on, and in how far you can customize beyond the standard features.

How to choose

  • New store, small team, want to launch quickly: Shopify.
  • Existing WordPress site with lots of content: WooCommerce.
  • Custom pricing, unusual product types or deep integrations: WooCommerce, or Shopify with a specialist developer.
  • No one to handle updates and security: Shopify, or WooCommerce with paid maintenance.

Understanding the real costs

Comparing platforms on the headline price alone is misleading. For Shopify, add the monthly plan, payment processing fees, any extra transaction fees and the paid apps you need, such as reviews, subscriptions or advanced filters. Apps are often billed monthly and can add up as the store grows. For WooCommerce, add hosting suitable for a store, a premium theme if used, paid extensions, a security service, backups and the time or freelancer cost for updates and maintenance.

Ask any developer you hire to list the expected monthly costs for your setup, not only the build price. Then compare the two platforms over a year or two, including the value of your own time.

Growing and changing later

Both platforms can grow with a successful store. Shopify offers higher plans with more features for larger businesses, while WooCommerce stores often move to stronger hosting and add custom development. Switching platforms is possible, but it means migrating products, customers, orders and redirects for old links, so it is worth choosing carefully at the start. If you expect complex needs later, such as wholesale pricing, multiple currencies or many integrations, mention them to developers now so they can recommend a setup that will not need replacing.

Who to hire

Look for a developer who specializes in your chosen platform and can show live stores. For Shopify, ask about theme customization, apps they recommend and how they keep the store fast with many apps installed. For WooCommerce, ask about hosting, caching, security, backups and how they test plugin updates. In both cases, ask how product data will be imported, how payments and taxes will be configured and who will test checkout before launch.

Launch checklist for either platform

  • Store and domain accounts are in your name.
  • Payments, shipping and tax settings are tested.
  • Legal pages, such as returns and privacy, are published.
  • Test orders go through and emails arrive.
  • Analytics and conversion tracking work.
  • You know how to add products and process orders.
