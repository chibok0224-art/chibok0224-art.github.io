Proofreading

Proofreading is the final check before publishing. A proofreader corrects spelling, grammar, punctuation, typos and small consistency issues, such as capital letters or number formats. They do not rewrite your sentences or change your structure. Proofreading is the right choice when the text is finished and you are happy with the content, but want it to be error-free.

Editing

Editing goes deeper. An editor improves clarity, flow and structure, cuts repetition, tightens sentences and flags gaps or confusing passages. There are different levels: light editing smooths the language, while heavier editing may reorganize sections or rewrite paragraphs. For books, developmental editing looks at the whole story or argument before line editing begins. Agree on the level before the work starts.

Copywriting

Copywriting means writing new text designed to make people act: buy, sign up, click or get in touch. Typical work includes sales pages, ads, product descriptions and emails. A copywriter starts from your offer and audience, not from an existing text, and focuses on benefits, objections and a clear call to action.

Content writing

Content writing produces informative or engaging text, such as blog posts, articles, guides and website pages. It aims to answer questions, build trust and often attract search traffic. Many writers do both copywriting and content writing, but the skills differ: persuasion and brevity on one side, research and depth on the other.

A useful test is to ask what you want a reader to do or feel after reading. If the answer is simply to understand clear, correct information, you probably need editing or proofreading. If you want them to take action, such as buying or booking, copywriting is the better fit. If you want them to learn something and trust you, content writing is usually right.

How to choose

  • Your text is final but may contain mistakes: proofreading.
  • Your draft is hard to follow or too long: editing.
  • You need a page that sells: sales copywriting.
  • You need articles or website pages from scratch: content writing.
  • You have a manuscript: book editing, then proofreading.

Common mistakes when choosing

  • Ordering proofreading for a draft that still needs structural work. The proofreader will fix commas, but the text will still be hard to follow.
  • Expecting a copywriter to know your product without a proper brief and examples.
  • Asking for heavy editing but being surprised when sentences change. Agree on the level first.
  • Skipping a final proofread after design, when new errors are easy to introduce.
  • Choosing on price per word alone. Specialized writing, such as technical or medical topics, needs subject knowledge.

If you are unsure which service fits, send a short sample to a writer and ask what they recommend. A good professional will tell you honestly whether your text needs a light proofread or deeper help, even if the answer is the smaller job.

What to tell the writer

For proofreading and editing, say which language variety you use, such as US or UK English, share any style guide and mark sections that must not change, such as legal wording. Ask for tracked changes so you can see every edit. For copywriting and content, share your audience, goal, tone, key messages and examples you like. Agree on word counts and the number of revisions before ordering.

Whatever you choose, plan a final proofread after design. Errors often appear when text is copied into a layout.
