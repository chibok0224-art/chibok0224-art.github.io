What a good audio editing package includes

Audio editing gigs are priced by length and the amount of cleanup. Check that the package states:

  • Cleanup: noise, hum, clicks, echo and breaths.
  • Editing: mistakes, pauses and filler words.
  • Levels: balanced speakers and consistent volume.
  • Loudness for your platform.
  • Formats and sample rates.
  • Revisions.
  • Turnaround.

Send a short, difficult section as a test before the full job. It shows how the editor handles your specific problems, such as background noise or echo, and whether the result still sounds natural.

Say how natural you want the result to sound. Some projects benefit from very clean, tightly edited audio; others, such as interviews or documentaries, sound better with some room tone and natural pauses. Agreeing on the style avoids audio that feels over-processed or too raw for the context, and it saves a round of revisions later.

How to prepare audio for editing

  1. Original files in the best quality.
  2. What needs fixing, with time stamps.
  3. Editing style: light or tight.
  4. Where it will be used: podcast, video, course, broadcast.
  5. Delivery format and loudness target.
  6. Deadline.

Improve future recordings where you can: a quieter room, soft furnishings, a closer microphone and separate tracks for each speaker. Every improvement at recording saves editing time and gives better results than any repair afterwards.

Keep the originals. Store unedited files separately, so you can always return to them if an edit needs to be redone or used for a different purpose.

For regular content, agree on a standard: loudness, how much to edit and file naming. Consistent settings make every episode or lesson sound the same.

What drives the price

  • length of audio
  • amount of noise and problems
  • number of speakers or tracks
  • editing depth
  • loudness and delivery standards
  • turnaround time

Heavily damaged audio takes much longer to repair. Ask for an estimate based on a sample rather than a fixed price per minute.

Red flags

  • Voices that sound robotic or underwater after cleanup.
  • Choppy edits with unnatural cuts.
  • No test on your difficult audio.
  • Compressed files returned when you asked for WAV.
  • Promises to remove any noise completely.

Listen to the edited audio on headphones and a phone speaker. Artifacts from noise reduction are often obvious on headphones but hidden on laptop speakers.

If you record regularly, ask the editor for feedback on your recordings. Simple advice about microphone placement, room choice or recording levels can reduce editing time and cost for every future episode, lesson or video. It is often the most valuable part of working with an experienced editor over time.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • I am sending original, best-quality files.
  • I have listed problems with time stamps.
  • I will test a difficult section first.
  • Delivery format and loudness are agreed.
  • I will keep the originals.
