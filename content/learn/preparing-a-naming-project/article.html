1. What the name must convey

Write a short description of your business or product, what makes it different and the feeling you want the name to create. Is it trustworthy and established, playful and friendly, premium and calm, or bold and technical? List three to five words that describe the brand personality. A brand strategist can help clarify this if you are unsure, and the result will also guide your logo and messaging later.

2. Audience and markets

Describe who will say, search for and remember the name. Note every country and language where you plan to operate. A name that sounds good in one language may be hard to pronounce, or carry an unfortunate meaning, in another. Ask the freelancer to flag any language issues, or have native speakers check the shortlist. If you plan to expand internationally, our translation vs localization article explains why this matters.

3. Types of names

Names generally fall into a few types:

  • Descriptive: says what you do. Clear, but often generic and harder to protect.
  • Suggestive: hints at a benefit or feeling. A common balance between clarity and distinctiveness.
  • Invented: a new word. Distinctive and easier to secure, but needs marketing to give it meaning.
  • Compound or borrowed: combines words or uses an existing word in a new context.

Say which types you are open to. If you have no preference, ask the freelancer to include a mix.

4. Practical limits

List requirements such as maximum length, whether the name must be easy to spell after hearing it once, and domain or social handle needs. Decide whether you need an exact-match domain or would accept a short addition. Mention names you already like or dislike, and names of competitors, so the freelancer avoids anything too similar.

5. Evaluating the shortlist

When you receive names, resist choosing by first impression alone. For each finalist:

  • Say it aloud and ask someone to spell it after hearing it.
  • Imagine it on your website, on packaging and in a sentence such as I just ordered from.
  • Search for it to see what already exists online.
  • Check domain and social handle availability.
  • Ask a few potential customers what it makes them think of.

New names often feel strange at first; familiarity grows quickly. Focus on whether the name fits the brand and is practical, rather than whether you love it immediately.

6. Availability and protection

Naming freelancers typically do basic availability searches, but they are not legal advisers. Before you invest in a logo, signage or packaging, have a trademark professional check your chosen name in the markets where you will operate. This step can prevent a costly rebrand later.

7. After choosing

Once you have a name, register the domain and social handles promptly, then move on to visual identity. A logo designer can build the look around the name, and a tagline can explain what an invented or suggestive name does not.

Common naming mistakes

Common mistakes include choosing a name that only makes sense with a long explanation, picking a spelling that people will get wrong after hearing it, and settling on a name so close to a competitor that customers confuse the two. Another is falling in love with a name before checking that the domain, handles and trademark are realistically available. Testing finalists against these points before deciding saves a great deal of frustration.

Naming brief checklist

  • Business description and what makes it different.
  • Brand personality words.
  • Audience, countries and languages.
  • Preferred name types.
  • Length, spelling, domain and handle requirements.
  • Competitors and names to avoid.
  • Plan for testing and professional availability checks.
