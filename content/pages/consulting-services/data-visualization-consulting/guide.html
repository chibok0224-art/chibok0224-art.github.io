What a good data visualization consultation includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Review scope.
  • Before-and-after examples.
  • Chart guidelines.
  • Dashboard recommendations.
  • Training.
  • Written summary.
  • Follow-up.

Ask for before-and-after redesigns of a few of your real charts. Seeing your own data improved is the clearest way to understand the advice.

Request a short reporting style guide with colors, chart types and labeling rules.

How to brief a data visualization consultant

  1. Reports and dashboards to review.
  2. Audience and decisions.
  3. Tools used.
  4. Problems you notice.
  5. Brand colors.
  6. Team skills.
  7. Deadline.

Explain who reads each report and what decision it supports. A chart for an executive meeting needs a different design from a detailed analysis for analysts.

Share examples of reports people find confusing. Real pain points lead to the most useful advice.

Mention accessibility needs, such as colorblind-friendly palettes and readable text sizes.

Ask consultants for examples of reporting standards they created and how teams adopted them.

Keep improved templates in a shared place for everyone.

Think about where reports are viewed. Charts shown on a large meeting screen, read on a phone or printed in a board pack need different layouts and text sizes. Telling the consultant how people actually consume reports leads to designs that work in practice, not only on a designer's monitor.

What drives the price

  • session length and number
  • review or audit depth
  • written report or roadmap
  • size and complexity of your systems
  • follow-up support
  • consultant experience

Reviewing a single dashboard costs much less than a full reporting standard with redesigns and team training.

Start with the reports used most often. Improving them brings the biggest benefit for the least effort.

Schedule a follow-up workshop after teams have used the guidelines for a few weeks.

Ask about telling a story with data. Good reports often lead with the key message, then show supporting charts and finally offer detail for those who need it. Learning this structure helps your team build presentations that guide decisions rather than simply displaying numbers.

Red flags

  • Decoration over clarity.
  • Ignores how reports are used.
  • Tools you cannot maintain.
  • No examples with your data.
  • Generic advice.

Review key reports after changes to confirm they are easier to use.

For building dashboards, see our dashboard guide and data visualization guide. For analysis, read our data analytics guide.

Ask for a simple checklist for new charts: title, labels, units, source and one clear message.

Ask whether the consultant can help simplify reports by removing rarely used charts. Fewer, clearer views often make dashboards more useful than adding new ones.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • I shared real reports.
  • Decisions and audiences are clear.
  • I will see before-and-after examples.
  • A style guide is included.
  • Advice fits my tools.
