What a good composing package includes

Composer gigs are priced by length, instrumentation and license. Check that the package states:

  • Length of music and number of tracks.
  • Instrumentation: virtual instruments, live players or both.
  • Mixing and final files.
  • Versions: full, short, loops and stems.
  • Revisions.
  • License: uses, territories, duration and exclusivity.
  • Delivery time.

Ask to hear a short sketch before the full track is produced. A rough version with the main idea and mood lets you give direction early, when changes are easy.

Listen to the composer's work in context, not only as standalone tracks. Scores in films, games or ads show whether they can support a scene without overpowering it, which is a different skill from writing an impressive piece of music on its own.

How to brief a composer

  1. The project: film, game, ad, podcast or video.
  2. The video or scene, with a locked edit if possible.
  3. Mood and story at each moment.
  4. Reference tracks, with what you like in each.
  5. Length and cues: where music starts and stops.
  6. How it will be used and where it will be published.
  7. Deadline.

Give feedback in musical terms when you can, with time stamps: more tension from 0:40, fewer drums in the dialogue, a bigger ending. Composers can act on specific notes far faster than on general feelings.

For games, ask for music that loops cleanly and for stems or intensity layers if the music should change with gameplay. These technical needs affect how the music is written from the start.

Keep dialogue and sound effects in mind. Music that is too busy under speech makes a video harder to follow, so share the final mix context with the composer.

What drives the price

  • length and number of tracks
  • live musicians versus virtual instruments
  • complexity of the arrangement
  • stems, loops and versions
  • license scope and exclusivity
  • the composer's experience and credits

Exclusive, broad licenses cost more than non-exclusive licenses for online use. Choose the license that matches your real needs.

Red flags

  • Vague licenses or no written terms.
  • Music that closely imitates famous scores.
  • No sketch stage before full production.
  • Only one version when you need edits.
  • Samples with poor sound quality.

Register or document your license and keep it with the project files. Platforms sometimes flag music automatically, and proof of your license helps resolve claims quickly.

Tips for a smoother project

Think about future use. If you may need more music later, such as sequels, updates or new episodes, keep the project files and ask whether the composer can extend the same themes. A consistent musical identity across releases helps audiences recognize your work.

Questions to ask before you order

  • Will you send a short sketch before producing the full track?
  • Which license will I get, and does it cover my type of use and distribution?
  • Will I receive alternate versions, such as shorter edits, loops or stems?
  • Is the music original, rather than closely based on a famous score?
  • How many revision rounds are included?

Send a reference track and a video or scene description if the music is for picture. Telling the composer how the music should make people feel works better than technical terms.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • I have reference tracks and time-stamped notes.
  • The video or cues are locked.
  • I will hear a sketch before full production.
  • The license covers every use in writing.
  • I will get the versions and stems I need.
