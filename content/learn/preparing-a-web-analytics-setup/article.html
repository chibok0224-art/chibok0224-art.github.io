1. Write the questions you want answered

Good analytics starts with questions, not tools. Examples: which channels bring paying customers, which pages lose visitors, how many enquiries come from mobile, and which ad campaign is worth its cost. Write down five or six questions in plain language. The specialist can then decide which events and reports are needed instead of tracking everything.

2. List the actions to track

Name the visitor actions that matter: purchases, form submissions, phone-number taps, booking requests, downloads, newsletter sign-ups and video plays. Rank them by importance. For an online shop, ask for details such as product views, basket additions and the value of each order. For a service site, a few clear conversion events are usually enough.

3. Include your marketing sources

List where visitors come from now or will come from: search, social media, email, paid ads and referrals. If you run paid campaigns, tell the specialist so that links can be tagged consistently and ad platforms can be connected. Without consistent tagging, visits from different campaigns end up mixed together and you cannot compare them.

4. Sort out accounts and access

Make sure you own the analytics account, the tag manager account and the ad accounts. Give the specialist the access they need and no more, and remember to remove it when the job ends. Note who can change the website, since some tracking requires a change to the site itself, and who will approve changes.

5. Think about privacy and consent

Visitors in many places have the right to choose whether tracking is used. Tell the specialist where your audience lives and which consent tool, if any, you already use. Ask them to configure tracking so it respects the choices visitors make, and to document what is collected. A short privacy policy that matches what you track is also important.

6. Ask for testing and a handover

Ask the specialist to test each tracked event and show you where it appears in the reports. Request a short walkthrough of the reports that matter most, plus written notes listing what is tracked, how it is named and where the tags live. A simple dashboard with your key numbers saves time every month.

Questions to ask the specialist

  • Which tools do you recommend for my situation, and why?
  • How will you name events so the reports stay readable?
  • How will you set up consent so that tracking respects visitor choices?
  • Will I receive written documentation of what is tracked?
  • Can you connect my advertising accounts and set up conversions?

After the setup

Make a habit of looking at a small set of numbers on a regular schedule, such as monthly. Pick three or four measures that match your goals, for example visits from each source, conversion rate and revenue per channel. Resist the urge to check every report every day. When you change the website or launch a campaign, tell whoever manages the tracking so that nothing breaks, and test the key events again after major site changes. Review the setup once a year to remove tags and events you no longer use.

Budget and timing

A basic setup for a small site can often be done in a short project, while an online shop with many events, several ad platforms and consent management takes longer. Ask for a fixed scope listing the events, platforms and reports included. Plan time to test after launch and a short review a few weeks later, when real data shows whether anything is missing. Keeping the setup simple and documented usually costs less than rebuilding a messy one later.

Common mistakes

  • Installing the tool but never defining what counts as success.
  • Tracking dozens of events that nobody looks at.
  • Letting the specialist own the accounts.
  • Skipping consent settings that your visitors may be entitled to.
