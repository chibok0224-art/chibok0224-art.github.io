1. Define the goal

Decide what the videos should achieve: sales through ads, trust on product pages, awareness on social media or content for your own channels. Choose a main measure, such as cost per purchase or watch time on the first three seconds. The goal affects the video length, the style and how many versions you need, and it tells you later which videos worked.

2. Describe your customer

Write who the product is for and what problem it solves, in the words customers use. Real quotes from reviews and messages are very helpful. Think about the creator too: someone your customers can relate to, with a similar age, lifestyle or situation, often performs better than a creator who looks like a professional model.

3. Choose the message and hooks

Pick one main message and a few hooks, the opening lines that stop viewers from scrolling. Examples include a common problem, a surprising result, a question or a simple demonstration. Suggest hook ideas but allow creators to adapt them to their own voice, because forced scripts feel fake. List claims that must be accurate and those you may not make.

4. Handle product and logistics

Plan how the product reaches the creator, who pays shipping and how long it takes. Include clear use instructions, so that creators demonstrate it correctly. For digital products, provide access and a short guide. Remember that delivery time is part of your schedule, and that returns or replacements may be needed.

5. Write a short brief

Keep the brief to one page: the audience, the main message, must-say and must-not-say points, examples of videos you like, the video length, the format such as vertical, and whether captions are needed. Share your brand name pronunciation and any visuals to include or avoid. Give creators room for personality within clear limits.

6. Agree rights and disclosure

State where you will use the videos: organic posts, paid ads, your website, email or marketplaces, and for how long. Broader rights usually cost more. Ask the creator to confirm that music and images in the video are licensed for your use. Check the advertising and disclosure rules that apply where you sell, and ask creators to follow them. When in doubt, ask a qualified professional.

Questions to ask creators

  • Can you show videos you made for products like mine?
  • How many hook variations can you film at once?
  • Do I receive raw footage, and may I edit and add captions?
  • Which usage rights are included, and for how long?
  • How do you handle product claims and disclosure rules?
  • How quickly can you deliver once the product arrives?

Testing and learning

Launch the videos in a small test, compare the numbers and keep notes on which hooks, creators and lengths performed best. Ask creators for new versions based on what worked, and retire the weak ones quickly. A steady loop of testing and new variations usually beats a single big video, because audiences tire of the same ad.

Budget and timing

Costs depend on the number of videos, the creator's experience, editing and the rights you need. Plan for product shipping, any extra editing and a testing budget for ads. Allow several weeks for sourcing creators, shipping the product, filming, revisions and testing. Begin with a modest batch, then place a larger order with the creators and styles that performed best, which is usually more efficient than a large first order.

Reviewing the videos

Watch each video on a phone with the sound off first, then with sound. Check that the first seconds grab attention, the product is shown clearly, captions are readable and every claim is true and allowed. Compare the footage with your brief and give one organized list of requested changes. Keep a record of approvals and usage rights for each creator, so you always know where each video may run.

Common mistakes

  • Over-scripting so the video feels like an advertisement.
  • Not checking usage rights for paid ads.
  • Ordering one video and expecting it to carry the campaign.
  • Making claims about the product that you cannot support.
