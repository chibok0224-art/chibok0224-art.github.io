1. Define the book

Write down the purpose of the book: build authority, tell a life story, teach a method, support a business or leave a record for family. Say who the readers are and what you want them to feel or do afterward. Decide the approximate length and the type: memoir, business book, how-to guide or fiction. A clear one-paragraph summary helps a ghostwriter judge whether they are a good fit and quote accurately.

2. Gather your material

Collect what you already have: notes, talks, blog posts, presentations, interviews, emails and stories. If you have a rough outline, share it. Think about which stories show your message best, and which facts, names or dates the writer must get right. If you prefer to talk rather than write, plan recorded interviews, since many ghostwriters turn conversations into chapters.

3. Describe your voice

Share examples of books you like and writing that sounds like you. Describe your tone: warm, direct, humorous, formal. Mention words and phrases you use often and ones you dislike. A short sample of your own writing or a recording helps the ghostwriter capture how you sound. Plan to read early pages and give honest feedback, since voice problems are easiest to fix at the start.

4. Agree the process

Ask how the work will run: an outline first, then chapters, with your review after each stage. Agree how many interviews are included, how long each takes and how revisions are handled. Decide how files are shared and how you will communicate. A written plan with dates for the outline, first chapters and full draft keeps both sides on track and shows early whether the working style suits you.

5. Settle rights and credit

Put in writing who owns the text, whether the ghostwriter may mention the project and whether they receive any credit, such as an acknowledgment. Confirm that the work is original and that any research or quotes are properly sourced. Agree on confidentiality, since you may share private stories and business details. For anything legal, consult a qualified professional to review the contract.

6. Plan editing and next steps

Ask whether the price includes editing and proofreading, or whether you will hire a separate editor. Decide who handles the cover, interior design and publishing. Planning these steps early avoids delays at the end, and a fresh editor reading the finished manuscript often catches issues that the writer and author no longer see.

Questions to ask the ghostwriter

  • Which books like mine have you written, and can I read samples?
  • How do you capture a client's voice?
  • What is your process and schedule from outline to final draft?
  • How many revisions and interviews are included?
  • Who owns the final text, and will that be in a written agreement?
  • How do you protect my material and keep it confidential?

Budget and payment

Book projects are priced by length, research needed, number of interviews and the writer's experience. Prefer a payment plan tied to milestones, for example a portion at the start, at the outline, at the first half and at final delivery, rather than all at once. Ask for a sample chapter or a paid trial section before committing to the whole book, and keep a reserve for editing and design.

Reviewing drafts well

Read each chapter soon after you receive it and answer in one organized message. Mark places where the facts are wrong, the tone is off or a story is missing, and say what you want instead of only that it feels wrong. Keep a running list of decisions, such as names, dates and spellings, so that the writer does not have to ask twice. Your quick, specific feedback is the biggest factor in finishing on schedule and in a voice that sounds like you.

Common mistakes

  • Starting without a clear purpose or reader in mind.
  • Not setting aside time for interviews and feedback.
  • Skipping a written agreement on rights and credit.
  • Paying everything up front without milestones.
