What a good game concept package includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Core loop and key mechanics.
  • Game design document length and sections.
  • Platform and controls.
  • Progression and monetization, if relevant.
  • Scope estimate for development.
  • Ownership of ideas.
  • Revisions and delivery time.

Ask for a one-page summary in addition to the full document. A short pitch makes it easy to share the idea with artists, developers, publishers or investors.

Request a list of features for a first playable prototype. Building the smallest fun version first is the fastest way to learn whether the idea works.

Look at concepts the designer has created and, if possible, games that were built from them. A clear document that a team actually used is better evidence of skill than many ambitious ideas that never reached a playable version.

How to brief a game concept designer

  1. Your idea and inspiration.
  2. Target players.
  3. Platform and controls.
  4. Team size and budget.
  5. Genre and mood.
  6. Business model: premium, free-to-play, other.
  7. Deadline.

Share your team and budget honestly. A designer can scale the concept to what you can actually build, which is far more valuable than an exciting plan you cannot finish.

Describe the feeling you want players to have: tense, relaxed, clever or powerful. Mechanics should serve that feeling, and naming it helps the designer make consistent choices.

Ask for reasons behind each mechanic. Knowing why a rule exists helps your team keep the design coherent when they adjust details during development.

Plan to test early with real players. Even rough paper or digital prototypes reveal whether the core loop is fun, long before expensive art and polish.

What drives the price

  • concept depth
  • document length
  • number of mechanics and systems
  • monetization design
  • level design
  • rush delivery

A one-page concept with a core loop costs much less than a full design document with systems, levels and monetization details.

Ongoing design support during development is usually a separate arrangement.

Red flags

  • Concepts copied from popular games.
  • Huge scope for a small team.
  • No core loop explanation.
  • Long documents without decisions.
  • Unclear ownership.

Keep the design document updated as the game changes. A living document prevents confusion when team members join later.

Tips for a smoother project

Store early versions too. They show how the design evolved and why decisions were made.

Share the concept with a few people from your target audience, such as players of similar games. Their reaction to a short pitch tells you whether the idea is exciting and easy to understand before you invest in development.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • Team size and budget are shared.
  • The core loop is clear.
  • The concept fits my platform.
  • A prototype feature list is included.
  • I own the documents.
