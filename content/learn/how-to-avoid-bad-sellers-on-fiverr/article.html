The short version

Look at samples that match your project, read recent reviews from buyers like you, send one specific question before ordering, keep everything on the platform, and start with a small order. Those five habits filter out most bad sellers. The rest of this article explains what to look for at each stage.

Red flags before you order

  • A portfolio that doesn't match the job. If you need a minimalist tech logo and every sample is a vintage badge, the seller may not be the right fit, however good they are.
  • Samples that look like they came from different people. Wildly inconsistent styles can mean the work isn't all theirs.
  • Promises that sound too good. Guaranteed rankings, unlimited revisions at a very low price, or delivery far faster than everyone else.
  • Vague package descriptions. No mention of what files you get, how many revisions are included or what counts as a revision.
  • Copy-paste replies. A generic answer to a specific question suggests they didn't read your message.
  • Requests to move off the platform. Asking to talk or pay elsewhere removes your protection and breaks Fiverr's rules.

Checks that take five minutes

  1. Filter by what you need. Use the category, delivery time and budget filters, and consider seller badges such as Vetted Pro or Top Rated to narrow the list.
  2. Read recent reviews, not just the average. Look for reviews from the last few months and from buyers with projects like yours. Note how the seller responded to any criticism.
  3. Compare packages line by line. Concepts or quantity, revisions, files, commercial use and delivery time. Our article on understanding packages explains what to compare.
  4. Send one specific question. For example, "Can you deliver an SVG and a transparent PNG?" or "Have you edited podcasts with three speakers?" A clear, prompt answer is a good sign.
  5. Start small. For a big or ongoing project, order a small paid test first.

Red flags during the order

  • Missing the first agreed milestone without telling you.
  • Refusing to show any work in progress on a long project.
  • Arguing about things that were clearly written in your brief.
  • Asking for extra payment for something the package already lists.

If one of these happens, raise it early and in writing on the order page. It is much easier to fix the direction at the first draft than at the final delivery. A clear brief helps too; see how to write a brief.

Red flags at delivery

  • Files or formats you were promised are missing.
  • Fonts, images or music that may not be licensed for your use.
  • Work that looks like a lightly edited template.
  • Holding back files or access until you pay more.

Don't accept the delivery until you have checked it. Open every file, compare it with your brief, and check it on the device your audience will use. Our article on checking a delivery has a full checklist.

If something goes wrong

  1. Request a revision with specific, polite feedback: what is wrong, where, and why it matters.
  2. If the revision doesn't fix it, message the seller and propose a solution, such as another round or a partial redo.
  3. If you can't agree, use the options on the order page and contact Fiverr's customer support. Keep all communication on the platform so there is a record.

Being a good buyer helps

Many orders go wrong because the brief was unclear, not because the seller was bad. Give examples, describe what "done" looks like, answer questions quickly and give feedback the seller can act on. Good sellers notice good buyers, and often go further for them.
