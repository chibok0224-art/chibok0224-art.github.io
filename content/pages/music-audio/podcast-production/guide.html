What a good podcast production package includes

Podcast gigs are usually priced per episode and by recording length. Check that the package states:

  • Editing: removing mistakes, long pauses, filler words and background noise.
  • Mixing: balanced voice levels, and loudness suitable for podcast apps.
  • Intro, outro and music, and whether the music is licensed.
  • Length limit of raw audio per episode.
  • Extras: show notes, transcripts, chapter markers, audiograms or video clips.
  • Publishing: whether the editor uploads episodes to your hosting service.
  • Turnaround per episode and revisions.

How to prepare for a podcast editor

  1. Raw files: one track per speaker if possible, in a high-quality format.
  2. Editing style: light or tight, with a reference episode from a show you like.
  3. Intro and outro audio, music and any fixed announcements.
  4. Notes with time stamps for parts to cut or keep.
  5. Show details for notes and publishing: guest names, links and episode titles.
  6. Your schedule, so turnaround can be planned.

Better recordings save more time than any editing. A decent microphone, a quiet room with soft surfaces and each speaker recorded separately will improve the result more than extra editing rounds.

Start with one paid test episode. Listen on headphones and in the car, then give specific feedback with time stamps. Once the style is set, write it down so every later episode sounds the same.

What drives the price

  • length of the raw recording
  • number of speakers and tracks
  • light versus detailed editing
  • noise repair for poor recordings
  • show notes, transcripts and video clips
  • publishing and monthly packages versus single episodes

Red flags

  • No before-and-after samples.
  • Music with no license information.
  • Very fast turnaround at a very low price, suggesting automatic processing only.
  • Requests for your hosting account password instead of team access.
  • No clear limit on raw audio length, leading to surprise charges.

Agree on a simple file routine from the start: where you upload raw recordings, how files are named, and where finished episodes are delivered. A shared folder with one sub-folder per episode works well. It prevents lost files and mix-ups, and makes it easy to switch editors later if you need to. Keep your raw recordings as a backup after each episode is published, at least until it has been live for a while.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • I can send one track per speaker in good quality.
  • I have described my editing style with a reference episode.
  • My intro and outro music is licensed.
  • I know which extras are included and which cost more.
  • I will start with a single test episode.
