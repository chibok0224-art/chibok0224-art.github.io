What a good project management package includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Scope: which project and phases.
  • Deliverables: plan, timeline, risk list, reports.
  • Tools and access.
  • Meetings and reporting rhythm.
  • Availability and response times.
  • Decision rights.
  • Duration and pricing model.

Ask for a sample status report from a past project with details removed. Good reports are short, show progress against plan, list risks honestly and say what decisions are needed.

Agree on a weekly rhythm early: when the team updates tasks, when the manager reports and when decisions are made. A predictable routine keeps projects moving without endless meetings.

Ask about a project that went wrong and how they handled it. Experienced managers can describe a missed deadline or budget problem calmly, explain what they changed and what they learned. Honest answers are a better sign than stories where everything always went perfectly.

How to brief a project manager

  1. The project goal and success criteria.
  2. Scope and deadline.
  3. Team members and vendors involved.
  4. Budget and constraints.
  5. Current status and known problems.
  6. Tools you use.
  7. Who decides what.

Define what done looks like. A clear list of outcomes, such as the site is live with these pages and payment working, prevents arguments about whether the project is finished.

Share known risks openly, such as a busy key person or an unreliable supplier. A good manager plans around risks, but only if they know about them.

Introduce the manager to the team properly and explain their role. People cooperate much better when they know the manager has your support and why they were hired.

Agree on how changes to scope are handled. New ideas appear during every project, and a simple process for recording requests, estimating impact and approving or postponing them keeps the original goals safe while still allowing sensible changes.

What drives the price

  • project size and complexity
  • number of people and vendors
  • hours per week
  • industry expertise
  • tools setup
  • duration

Part-time coordination of a small project costs much less than full-time management of a complex program with many teams and vendors.

Red flags

  • No questions about goals or success criteria.
  • Reports that only list completed tasks.
  • Heavy processes for a small project.
  • Unavailable during critical phases.
  • No experience with similar projects.

At the end, ask for a short closing review: what went well, what did not and what to do differently next time. It turns one project into lessons for the next.

Make sure the manager has the information and access they need from day one: the plan, contacts, tools and any contracts with vendors. Delays in access are a common reason new project managers lose their first week and the team loses confidence in them.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • Goals and done criteria are defined.
  • Decision rights are clear.
  • Reporting rhythm is agreed.
  • Known risks are shared.
  • Tools match what my team uses.
