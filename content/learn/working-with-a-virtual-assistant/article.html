1. Choose what to delegate

For one week, write down the tasks you do and how long each takes. Mark the ones that are repetitive, follow clear steps and do not require your personal judgment. These are ideal for a virtual assistant: scheduling, inbox sorting, data entry, research, updating spreadsheets, preparing reports, posting prepared social content and answering common customer questions. Start with two or three tasks rather than handing over everything at once.

2. Write simple instructions

Write step-by-step instructions for each task, even if they seem obvious to you. A short screen recording of you doing the task, with spoken explanation, is often the fastest way to create them. Include examples of finished work, common exceptions and what to do when something is unclear. These instructions become a reusable manual that makes it easy to train a new assistant later if needed.

3. Share access safely

Give your assistant their own user accounts wherever possible, with only the permissions they need. Many tools for email, calendars, documents and online stores support this. Where separate accounts are not available, use a password manager that can share access without revealing passwords. Avoid sharing banking or payment access unless truly necessary, and never send passwords in chat messages. When the work ends, remove access promptly.

4. Agree on hours and communication

Decide how many hours per week you need, which days and hours overlap with yours, and how quickly messages should be answered. Choose one main communication channel and a simple task list that both of you can see, so nothing gets lost in scattered messages. A brief daily or weekly update, listing what was done, what is pending and any questions, keeps you informed without micromanaging. Tools from project management can help as the work grows.

5. Start with a trial

Begin with a small, paid trial task that reflects real work. It shows how the assistant follows instructions, communicates and handles unexpected situations. Review the result together, explain what to adjust and update your instructions. Most assistants improve quickly once they understand your preferences.

6. Review and expand

After the first few weeks, check whether the delegated tasks are being done accurately and on time, and whether you are actually saving time. If so, add the next set of tasks. If not, look at the instructions and communication first; problems often come from unclear handovers rather than the assistant's ability. Give specific feedback and recognize good work.

7. Growing the relationship

A good virtual assistant becomes more valuable over time as they learn your business, your customers and your preferences. Invest in that knowledge. Share context about why tasks matter, not just how to do them, and invite suggestions for improving processes. Many assistants spot inefficiencies quickly because they see the work with fresh eyes. As trust grows, you can hand over larger responsibilities, such as managing a shared inbox, coordinating other freelancers or preparing weekly summaries. Review hours and rates periodically, and agree in advance how holidays, sick days and backup cover will be handled so that important tasks are never left waiting.

What to keep for yourself

  • Decisions that depend on your expertise or relationships.
  • Financial approvals and sensitive payments.
  • Final sign-off on anything public, at least at first.
  • Tasks you have not yet documented or explained.

Getting started checklist

  • List of repetitive tasks to delegate first.
  • Step-by-step instructions or screen recordings.
  • Separate accounts or safe password sharing.
  • Hours, response times and communication channel agreed.
  • Shared task list and regular updates.
  • Paid trial task and a review after the first weeks.
