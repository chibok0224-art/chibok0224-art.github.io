Logo makers

Logo makers let you choose a style, type a name and receive options built from templates and icons. They are fast, inexpensive and need no design skill. They suit very early projects, side businesses and temporary needs. The limits are originality and files: similar logos may appear for other businesses, and some tools provide small files or charge extra for versions you need in practice, such as a vector file for printing. Check the license terms carefully.

Hiring a designer

A designer starts by learning about your business, audience and competitors, then explores ideas and refines the one you choose. The result is more likely to be distinctive and to work across uses: signs, packaging, social media icons and tiny app icons. A good designer provides a full set of files and explains how to use them. The costs are higher, the process takes longer and you must give clear feedback.

How to choose

Think about how long you will use the logo and where. If you are testing an idea and may change the name, a simple logo is sensible. If you will print on products, build a reputation or face competitors with similar offers, a custom logo is a better investment. A middle path is to start with a simple wordmark and later hire a designer when the business is established.

Briefing a designer well

Share your business name, what you do, who your customers are and three to five logos you like and dislike, with reasons. Mention where the logo will appear and any colors or symbols to avoid. Ask what is included: number of concepts, revisions, file formats and a simple guide to colors and fonts. Confirm that you will own the final artwork and that it is original.

Questions to ask a designer

  • Can you show logos you designed for similar businesses?
  • How many concepts and revisions are included?
  • Which files will I receive?
  • Do I receive full rights to the final logo?
  • How do you make sure the design is original?
  • Do you offer a basic style guide?

Budget and timing

Logo prices vary widely with the designer's experience, the number of concepts and what else is included. Decide your budget before asking for quotes and compare what each quote contains: concepts, revisions, file types and usage rights. Allow time for feedback rounds. If you need something fast for a deadline, tell the designer early and ask what can be delivered in time. A simple, well-made logo now is better than a rushed complicated one.

After you have the logo

Store the final files in a shared folder with clear names and keep a copy of the invoice and agreement. Write down the exact colors and fonts so everyone who works with your brand uses them consistently. Use the logo with enough space around it, avoid stretching it and keep a version for dark and light backgrounds. If you grow, a designer can extend the logo into a full set of brand guidelines later.

Try this first

Spend an hour collecting inspiration: save ten logos you admire, write three words that describe your business and list where the logo will appear. Try one logo maker for free to see what a template can give you. Then compare it with the portfolios of two designers whose style you like. Seeing the difference between a template and a custom design makes it much easier to judge whether the extra investment is worth it for your business.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a logo that looks good large but fails at small sizes.
  • Skipping the check of files and usage rights.
  • Following trends that will look dated soon.
  • Not testing the logo on a dark background, a phone screen and in black and white.
