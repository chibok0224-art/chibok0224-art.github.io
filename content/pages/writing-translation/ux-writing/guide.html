What a good UX writing project includes

UX writing gigs range from reviewing a few screens to building a full content style guide. Check that the project states:

  • Scope: which screens, flows or messages.
  • Deliverables: text in the design file, a spreadsheet, or both.
  • Terminology: a list of key terms used consistently.
  • Tone guidelines.
  • Error and empty states.
  • Accessibility of labels and messages.
  • Revisions and collaboration with designers.

Ask the writer to start with the flows that matter most, such as sign-up, checkout or the first-time experience. Small wording changes there often have the biggest effect on whether people succeed.

Ask the writer to review whole flows, not single screens. The same term should mean the same thing from the first screen to the last, and buttons should match what happens next. Reviewing flows end to end catches inconsistencies that screen-by-screen editing misses.

How to brief a UX writer

  1. Designs or access to a test version.
  2. User goals for each flow.
  3. Known problems: support questions and drop-off points.
  4. Brand voice and existing guidelines.
  5. Technical limits: character counts and dynamic text.
  6. Languages, if the product will be localized.

Share support tickets and user feedback. They show exactly which words confuse people and where users get stuck, which is the best starting point for improving product text.

Test the new wording with a few users where possible. Watching someone complete a task with the new text quickly shows whether it is clearer than before.

What drives the price

  • number of screens and flows
  • research and testing
  • style guide or terminology creation
  • work in design files versus documents
  • localization preparation
  • the writer's experience with your type of product

A compact style guide is often worth the extra cost. It keeps future text consistent without hiring the writer for every small change.

Red flags

  • Samples that are clever but unclear.
  • No request to see designs or the product.
  • Error messages that blame the user.
  • Inconsistent terms across screens.
  • No thought for accessibility.

Good UX text is often invisible: users simply complete their task. Judge the work by fewer mistakes and support questions, not by how memorable the words are.

Keep product text in one place your team can update, such as a shared spreadsheet or the design file, rather than scattered across code. It makes future edits, translations and reviews much easier, and it lets a UX writer help again later without starting from scratch.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • The writer will see the designs or product.
  • I have shared user goals and known problems.
  • Key terms will be consistent across screens.
  • Error messages will be helpful and polite.
  • I will consider a short content style guide.
