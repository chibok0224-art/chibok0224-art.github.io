Fixed price

With a fixed price, you and the freelancer agree on what will be delivered and for how much. The cost is known in advance, and the freelancer carries the risk of taking longer than planned. This model works well for projects with a clear outcome: a logo, a set of illustrations, a translated document, a website with a defined list of pages. The weakness is that the scope must be described carefully. If it is vague, disagreements arise about what was included, and every change becomes a negotiation.

Hourly rate

With an hourly rate, you pay for the time spent. It suits work that is hard to define in advance: ongoing support, troubleshooting, research, an assistant who handles varied tasks, or a project that will evolve as you learn. It gives flexibility to change direction. The risk is cost: you may not know the total until the end, and you must trust the freelancer to work efficiently or check time reports carefully.

How to choose

Ask whether you can describe the finished result in a few sentences. If yes, a fixed price is usually the better fit. If the work depends on discoveries, feedback or varied requests, hourly pay or a monthly retainer may be easier. For a first project with a new freelancer, a small fixed-price trial is a low-risk way to judge quality and communication before committing to a larger arrangement.

Protecting yourself either way

For fixed-price work, write down what is included, how many revisions you get, what counts as a change and when you will pay. Consider paying in stages tied to milestones. For hourly work, agree on a budget cap, how time is tracked and how often you will receive a summary. In both cases, keep all agreements in writing and keep communication in one place so that you can refer back to it.

Questions to ask the freelancer

  • What exactly is included in the price?
  • How are changes to the scope handled and priced?
  • How many revision rounds are included?
  • For hourly work, how do you track and report time?
  • What is the payment schedule?
  • What happens if the project stops early?

Examples

A small business wants a five-page website. The pages, features and number of revisions are clear, so a fixed price works. Later, the same business needs help with updates, small fixes and questions as they arise; this is ongoing work with unpredictable tasks, so hourly pay or a small monthly retainer fits better. Another example is a translator paid per word, which is a fixed price per unit that scales with the amount of text. Choose the model that matches how clear the work is, not the one that sounds cheapest.

Building trust

Whichever model you use, good communication keeps costs fair. Respond promptly to questions, give feedback in organized lists and approve stages so work does not stall. Freelancers who feel respected are more likely to flag problems early, suggest cheaper options and deliver on time. A short written agreement protects both sides and costs nothing to prepare.

Try this first

Before you ask for quotes, write one page that describes the result you want, the deadline and your budget. Send it to two or three freelancers and ask each to propose a fixed price or an hourly estimate. Compare not only the numbers but how clearly each person understood your request and which questions they asked. A freelancer who asks good questions at this stage is usually one who will deliver well and prevent expensive misunderstandings later in the project.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the cheapest quote without comparing what is included.
  • Using a fixed price for work that is too vague to define.
  • Leaving hourly work open-ended with no budget cap.
  • Not writing down changes agreed during the project.
