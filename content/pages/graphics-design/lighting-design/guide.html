What a good lighting design package includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Spaces covered.
  • Lighting layout with positions.
  • Fixture schedule with models.
  • Brightness and color temperature.
  • Controls: dimmers, scenes, smart lighting.
  • Renders, if included.
  • Revisions and delivery time.

Ask for a fixture schedule with available products and alternatives. A plan with impossible-to-find fixtures is hard to build.

Request layered lighting: general light, task light and accent light. Layers let you change the mood for different activities.

Ask to see lighting plans and photos of finished projects. Real photos show whether the designer creates comfortable, even light or harsh glare and dark corners. Good lighting is often subtle, so look closely at how spaces feel rather than how bright they are.

How to brief a lighting designer

  1. Floor plans with measurements.
  2. Photos of the space.
  3. Use of each room.
  4. Mood you want.
  5. Existing wiring and constraints.
  6. Budget and suppliers.
  7. Deadline.

Describe activities in each space, such as cooking, reading, working or displaying products. Good lighting supports what people actually do.

Share ceiling heights and materials. They affect fixture choice, light spread and installation options.

Plan installation with a licensed electrician. Electrical safety rules apply, and the electrician should confirm the plan is safe and allowed.

Think about safety and accessibility. Well-lit steps, entrances, paths and stairs prevent accidents, and enough contrast helps people with low vision. These practical needs should be part of the plan alongside mood and decoration.

What drives the price

  • number of rooms or spaces
  • commercial versus residential
  • renders
  • smart controls
  • fixture sourcing
  • revisions

A simple plan for one room costs much less than a full commercial lighting design with renders, controls and fixture sourcing.

Request mockups or simple visualizations for key spaces, such as a dining area or shop window. Seeing light and shadow before buying fixtures helps you confirm the mood and avoid expensive changes after installation.

Red flags

  • No fixture schedule.
  • Ignores how spaces are used.
  • Mixed color temperatures without reason.
  • No mention of electrical safety.
  • Fixtures that cannot be bought locally.

Keep the fixture schedule for replacements. Matching bulbs and fittings later keeps the lighting consistent.

For rooms, see our interior design guide. For outdoors, read our landscape design guide and 3D architecture guide.

Consider energy use and maintenance. Efficient fixtures, long-lasting bulbs and simple controls reduce running costs. A designer can balance atmosphere with practical choices, such as easy-to-replace bulbs in high ceilings or outdoor fittings rated for weather.

If you use smart lighting, agree on scenes and naming before installation, such as morning, dinner and movie. Simple, well-named scenes are what people actually use every day, while complex setups often end up ignored after the first week.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • I shared floor plans and photos.
  • Each room's use is described.
  • The plan has a fixture schedule.
  • Lighting is layered.
  • A licensed electrician will install.
