What a good Android app project includes

Android gigs range from small apps to complex products. The project should state:

  • Features and screens for the first version.
  • Technology: native Kotlin or a cross-platform framework.
  • Minimum Android version and devices tested.
  • Backend and data storage.
  • Permissions and privacy handling.
  • Play Store publishing through your account.
  • Source code, signing keys and documentation.

Ask how the app signing key is managed. If the key is lost or kept by the developer, you may not be able to update your app. Google Play offers managed signing; make sure you understand and control the setup.

How to brief an Android developer

  1. What the app does and who it is for.
  2. Core features for the first release.
  3. Designs or wireframes.
  4. Target devices and countries.
  5. Backend needs: accounts, data, notifications.
  6. Monetization: ads, subscriptions or paid features.
  7. Launch date.

Share how your users actually use their phones: data limits, older devices, battery concerns. Apps built with these in mind feel faster and get better reviews, especially in markets where budget phones are common.

Install test builds yourself throughout the project. Most Android projects can share test versions through Google Play's testing tracks, so you and a few trusted users can try the app on real devices before release.

Plan the store listing alongside the app. Screenshots, a short description, a feature graphic and privacy details are all required, and they take longer than most people expect. Preparing them in parallel with development prevents a finished app from waiting days for its listing to be ready, and it lets you check that the listing promises only what the app actually does.

What drives the price

  • number of screens and features
  • native versus cross-platform
  • backend and integrations
  • device testing range
  • payments, subscriptions or ads
  • publishing support and maintenance

Plan for maintenance. Android and Google Play change their requirements regularly, and apps that are not updated can be hidden or removed from the store.

Red flags

  • The Play Store account in the developer's name.
  • Testing on one device only.
  • Unnecessary permissions.
  • No access to the signing key or source code.
  • No privacy policy for an app that collects data.

Read Google Play's policies for your type of app, especially if it handles payments, personal data, health or children's content. Rules change, and the responsibility for compliance stays with the app owner.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • The Play Store account is in my name.
  • Devices and Android versions to test are agreed.
  • Permissions are limited to what the app needs.
  • I control the signing setup and source code.
  • I have a privacy policy and plan for maintenance.
