Live-action video

Live-action means filming real people, products or locations. It is strong when the message depends on showing authenticity: a founder talking about why they started, customers describing results, a team at work, a product in real use. It builds familiarity because viewers see faces and places. Challenges include scheduling, location, lighting, sound and the fact that changes after filming can require a new shoot.

Animated video

Animation creates visuals from scratch, which makes it ideal for ideas that are hard to film: how software works, abstract processes, data, or a story that needs a stylized world. It can be quick to update, consistent across a series and flexible in style, from simple motion graphics to illustrated characters. It relies heavily on a good script and clear visual planning, and it may feel less personal than real faces unless the style and voice have warmth.

How to choose

Start with your message and your audience. If the main goal is trust in a person or a place, lean toward live-action. If the main goal is understanding a concept, lean toward animation. Consider where the video will live: short social clips often work well with fast animation or simple on-camera talking, while a company story may benefit from real faces. Think about how long the video must stay accurate, since a product that changes often favors a format that is easy to edit.

What to include in the brief

State the goal, the audience, the length, where it will be shown and the one thing viewers should do afterward. Share examples of videos you like and dislike. Provide a draft script or key points, brand colors and logo files, and any footage or assets you already have. Ask what is included: script help, voice, music, revisions and different sizes for different platforms.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • Can you show examples of similar videos for businesses like mine?
  • Do you help with the script, and how many revisions are included?
  • Who provides the voice or presenter, and can I approve it first?
  • What file formats and sizes will I receive for each platform?
  • Are music and stock footage licensed for commercial use?
  • Will you provide captions or the source files for later edits?

Planning the work

Most video projects follow the same steps: script, visual plan or storyboard, production, first cut, feedback and final delivery. Spend the most time on the first two, because they are cheap to change, while changes late in production are slow and costly. Keep the script short; a common mistake is trying to say everything in one video. Decide on one main message and one action for viewers.

Collect your materials before the work starts: logo, colors, fonts, product images and any footage. Name one person on your side who gives feedback, so that the freelancer receives a single, consistent set of comments instead of conflicting ones. After delivery, keep the final files and source files in a safe place so that you can update the video later without starting again.

Budgeting for video

Ask for a quote that lists what is included, then compare like with like. Short, focused videos usually cost less than long ones, and reusing one script across several sizes and platforms stretches the budget. Keep a small reserve for revisions or a shorter cut for social media, since you will likely want both once the main video is ready.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing a style because it is fashionable, not because it fits the message.
  • Writing a script that is too long for the planned video length.
  • Forgetting captions, since many people watch without sound.
  • Not planning how the video will be updated when the business changes.
