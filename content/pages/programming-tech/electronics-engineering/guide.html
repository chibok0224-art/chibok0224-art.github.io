What a good electronics package includes

Check that the offer clearly states:

  • Requirements and specifications.
  • Schematic and PCB design.
  • Bill of materials.
  • Firmware, if included.
  • Prototype testing.
  • Manufacturing files.
  • Ownership and timeline.

Ask for a design review before ordering boards. Catching mistakes on screen is far cheaper than discovering them after manufacturing.

Request alternative parts for key components. Supply problems can delay production for months.

Ask engineers about products they have taken from prototype to production. Experience with manufacturing, testing and certification is very different from building one-off hobby projects, and it saves time and money.

Plan for testing at each stage: power, signals, temperature and real use. Simple test plans catch problems before they reach customers and make certification smoother.

How to brief an electronics engineer

  1. What the device does.
  2. Power source and battery life.
  3. Size and enclosure limits.
  4. Connectivity.
  5. Target cost and quantities.
  6. Markets and certifications.
  7. Deadline.

Write a short requirements document with must-have and nice-to-have features. Clear priorities help the engineer make sensible trade-offs.

Plan certification early if the product uses mains power, radio or will be sold widely. Designing with certification in mind avoids expensive redesigns.

Think about enclosure and assembly early. The board must fit the case, connectors must line up and the product must be easy to assemble. Coordinating electronics and mechanical design avoids late redesigns.

Discuss power consumption early if the device runs on batteries. Component choices, sleep modes and firmware design decide battery life, and changing them late often means redesigning the board.

What drives the price

  • circuit complexity
  • number of board revisions
  • firmware development
  • component sourcing
  • prototype assembly
  • certification support

A simple sensor board costs much less than a wireless product with firmware, multiple revisions and certification support.

Consider repairability and updates. Accessible connectors, firmware update options and clear labeling make products easier to support after sale.

Ask for test points and labels on the prototype board. They make debugging easier for you, the engineer and any manufacturer who tests boards later.

Red flags

  • No design review.
  • Obscure or unavailable components.
  • Ignores safety and certification.
  • No manufacturing files.
  • Unclear ownership.

Keep all design files, test results and part lists versioned. They are essential for production and future updates.

Ask for a short design summary explaining key choices, so future engineers understand why the product was built this way.

Order a small batch of prototype boards before committing to larger quantities. Real boards reveal issues with assembly, heat and connectors that simulations miss, and small batches keep the cost of mistakes low while you refine the design.

Quick pre-order checklist

  • Requirements are written down.
  • A design review happens before ordering.
  • Parts have alternatives.
  • Certification is planned.
  • I own the design files.
