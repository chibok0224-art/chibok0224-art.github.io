1. Define learning goals

Start with what learners should be able to do after the course, written as actions: set up an account, write a project plan, use a tool safely. Clear goals keep the course focused and make it easier to decide what to cut. Describe the learners too: their current knowledge, why they are taking the course and how much time they have. A course for busy professionals needs short, practical lessons; a course for beginners needs more explanation and examples.

2. Build an outline

Group the content into modules, and each module into short lessons that cover one idea. For each lesson, note the key points, an example and a small activity. An e-learning content developer or instructional designer can turn your expertise into a structured outline, suggest activities and spot gaps. This is the best stage to involve them, because changes to an outline are quick and cheap.

3. Write scripts and materials

Script each video lesson, even if you will speak naturally on camera. Scripts keep videos short and make captions and translations easier later. Prepare supporting materials such as worksheets, checklists, templates and reading notes. A technical writer can help with step-by-step instructions for software or processes.

4. Design slides and visuals

Consistent, clean slides help learners follow along. Ask a presentation designer for a reusable template with layouts for titles, key points, diagrams and examples. Diagrams and simple animations can explain processes better than text. For difficult concepts, a short animated explainer may be worth the investment.

5. Record and edit video

Common formats are talking-head videos, screen recordings and slide-based narration. Choose formats that suit each lesson. Software tutorials work well as screencasts; introductions and stories often benefit from seeing the instructor. Good audio is the most important technical factor, so use a decent microphone and a quiet room. An e-learning video producer can edit lessons, add titles, zooms and captions, and keep the style consistent across the course.

6. Add activities and quizzes

Learners remember more when they practise. Add short quizzes after lessons, exercises that apply the skill and, where possible, a final project. Quiz results also show you which lessons are unclear. Keep questions focused on the learning goals rather than trivia.

7. Set up the platform

Choose a platform based on how you will sell or deliver the course, whether you need certificates, progress tracking or integration with other systems. Upload lessons, set up quizzes and test the full learner journey yourself, including sign-up, payment if relevant, completion and certificates. Add captions to improve accessibility and help learners who watch without sound.

8. Launch, listen and update

Run the course with a small group first and collect feedback. Look at where learners stop, which quiz questions they miss and what questions they ask. Short lessons make updates easy: you can replace a single video instead of re-recording an hour. Keep your scripts, slides and project files organized so updates stay quick.

Where to spend first

If budget is limited, invest first in a clear outline and good audio. Learners forgive simple visuals, but they quickly abandon lessons that are confusing or hard to hear. Polished animation and custom graphics can be added later to the lessons people value most.
